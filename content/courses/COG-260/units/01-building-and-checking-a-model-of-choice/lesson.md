## What a model of choice is for

Wilson and Collins (2019) name four uses of a computational model of behaviour: simulation, parameter estimation, model comparison and inference about latent variables. This unit runs the first three on one task, in the order their rules prescribe, and adds the step their worked example leaves out: a comparison on held-out data under a stated information contract. Every participant in this unit is synthetic, generated by the laboratory from a stated model and a seed. Nothing here measures a person.

The paper's rules are unnumbered section headings, and its text calls them "10 simple steps". This unit numbers them in heading order, as the source record does: rule 3 is "Simulate, simulate, simulate!", rule 5 is parameter recovery, rule 6 is model comparison and model recovery, and rule 8 is "Validate (at least) the winning model". The paper's own cross-reference to validation says "Step 9", although validation is the eighth heading; this unit's "rule 8" always means the validation heading.

## The task

The worked example is a two-armed bandit. On each of $T$ trials the participant chooses arm 0 or arm 1, and the chosen arm pays a reward of 1 with a fixed probability and 0 otherwise. The paper uses $T=1000$ and reward probabilities $0.2$ and $0.8$. The laboratory offers two cases built on the same task:

- the **informative case**: $T=300$, reward probabilities $0.2$ and $0.8$, and synthetic participants whose choices depend strongly on what they learn;
- the **ambiguous case**: $T=60$, reward probabilities $0.4$ and $0.6$, and participants whose choices barely depend on learned values.

Write $c_t\in\{0,1\}$ for the choice and $r_t\in\{0,1\}$ for the reward on trial $t$.

## Three models

Box 1 of the paper sets out five models for this task. This unit uses the first three.

**Model (biased random responding).** Each choice ignores history: $P(c_t=1)=b$ on every trial, with one parameter $b\in[0,1]$. It is the baseline. A model that claims to capture learning must predict choices better than this.

**Model (noisy win-stay lose-shift).** On trial 1 the choice is a coin flip. On later trials the participant follows the rule "repeat after a reward, switch after no reward" with probability $1-\varepsilon$ and chooses at random otherwise, so

$$P(c_t=c_{t-1}\mid r_{t-1}=1)=1-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2},\qquad P(c_t\neq c_{t-1}\mid r_{t-1}=0)=1-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}.$$

There is one parameter, $\varepsilon\in[0,1]$. Nothing is learned across trials: only the previous trial matters.

**Model (Rescorla-Wagner learning with softmax choice).** The participant keeps a value $Q_k$ for each arm, starting at $Q_0=Q_1=0.5$. After trial $t$ only the chosen arm is updated towards the outcome,

$$Q_{c_t}\leftarrow Q_{c_t}+\alpha\,(r_t-Q_{c_t}),$$

and choices follow the softmax rule

$$P(c_t=k)=\frac{e^{\beta Q_k}}{\sum_j e^{\beta Q_j}},\qquad\text{which for two arms is}\qquad P(c_t=1)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta(Q_1-Q_0)}}.$$

There are two parameters: the learning rate $\alpha\in[0,1]$ and the inverse temperature $\beta\ge0$. With $\beta=0$ choice ignores the values entirely. As $\beta$ grows, choice becomes nearly deterministic in favour of the higher value.

**Where the paper's text and its code differ.** Read Box 1 with the paper's code beside it (a MATLAB repository under the MIT licence). Three differences change numbers, though none changes the argument:

1. Box 1 states that the initial values are zero. The repository's simulation and likelihood functions start both values at $0.5$, and Box 6 uses $0.5$ for its own simulation. This unit and the laboratory start at $0.5$.
2. Equation 3 is printed as an update of "the value of option $k$", without restricting it to the chosen option. The repository updates only the chosen arm, as above.
3. Box 4 calls $\beta$ the softmax "temperature", while Box 1 defines it as the inverse temperature. Here $\beta$ is always the inverse temperature: a larger $\beta$ means more deterministic choice.

A model is not specified until its initial conditions and its update rule are, and anyone reproducing a result needs both.

## Predict before you fit

Rule 3 says to simulate before fitting anything, and to look first at *model-independent* measures: quantities computed from the choices without assuming any model. Box 2 uses two of them. One is the probability of repeating the previous choice, split by whether that choice was rewarded. The other is the probability of choosing the better arm in the first and the last trials of the session. The laboratory reports both, with the repeat probabilities also split by which arm was chosen, once your prediction is saved.

The three models make different predictions about these measures:

- **Biased responding** says that the previous reward makes no difference once the previous choice is held fixed: after a choice of arm 1 the next choice repeats it with probability $b$, and after arm 0 with probability $1-b$, whatever the reward. Split by reward alone, the repeat probabilities still differ whenever $b\neq\frac12$, because rewards come mostly from the better arm, so read that split together with the split by arm. The model predicts no learning curve.
- **Win-stay lose-shift** predicts a repeat probability of $1-\varepsilon/2$ after a reward and $\varepsilon/2$ after no reward, whatever happened earlier in the session.
- **Rescorla-Wagner** predicts more repeats after a reward than after no reward, by an amount that grows with $\alpha$ and $\beta$, and a learning curve in which the better arm is chosen more often late than early.

Write these predictions down for the design and parameter region you are about to run, *before* running the laboratory. The laboratory keeps its results hidden until a prediction has been saved in your notebook. The saved prediction is part of your record, and a prediction that turned out to be wrong is as useful in the report as one that was right.

## Fitting by maximum likelihood

Rule 4 fits each model by maximising the probability of the observed choices. For parameters $\theta$,

$$\ell(\theta)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ln P(c_t\mid c_{1:t-1},r_{1:t-1},\theta),$$

and the estimate $\hat\theta$ maximises $\ell$. Equivalently, it minimises the negative log-likelihood $\mathrm{NLL}=-\ell$.

**Biased responding.** If $n_1$ of the $T$ choices are arm 1, then $\ell(b)=n_1\ln b+(T-n_1)\ln(1-b)$, which is maximised at $\hat b=n_1/T$.

**Noisy win-stay lose-shift.** Trial 1 contributes $\ln\frac12$ whatever $\varepsilon$ is. Of the $n=T-1$ later trials, let $c$ be the number whose choice follows the rule. Each of those has probability $1-\varepsilon/2$, and each of the other $n-c$ has probability $\varepsilon/2$, so

$$\ell(\varepsilon)=\ln\tfrac12+c\ln\!\big(1-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\big)+(n-c)\ln\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}.$$

This also has a closed-form maximum in $n$ and $c$, which exercise 1 asks you to derive. Check where the maximum falls when fewer than half of the later trials follow the rule.

**Rescorla-Wagner.** There is no closed form. The values on each trial depend on $\alpha$ and on every earlier choice and reward, so $\ell(\alpha,\beta)$ is computed by running the update along the observed sequence and summing the log-probabilities of the choices actually made. The laboratory evaluates $\ell$ on a grid of $\alpha$ from 0 to 1 and $\beta$ up to 20, then refines the best grid point. Rule 4's practical warnings apply:

- keep log-likelihoods finite, since a predicted probability of exactly 0 for a choice that occurred makes $\ell=-\infty$;
- respect the parameter bounds;
- guard against local maxima by starting from several points. Box 3 shows several local maxima in a simplified reinforcement-learning and working-memory model.

The laboratory's alpha profile plots the negative log-likelihood against $\alpha$ for a fixed $\beta$. Read it for its shape. When $\beta$ is near zero the profile is flat, because choices do not depend on the values that $\alpha$ controls. A flat likelihood is the first sign that the data cannot pin a parameter down.

## Parameter recovery

Rule 5 says that before fitting real data you should check that the procedure recovers parameters you know. Simulate participants with chosen parameter values, fit the model to each simulated session, and plot the recovered values against the true ones. Box 4 does this for the Rescorla-Wagner model with $T=1000$, reward probabilities $0.2$ and $0.8$, $\alpha$ drawn uniformly from $[0,1]$ and $\beta$ drawn from an exponential distribution, repeated 1000 times. The paper writes the distribution as Exp(10), and the repository's call uses 10 as the mean.

Look for three things in a recovery plot:

1. **Scatter around the identity line.** Large scatter means the data do not determine the parameter.
2. **Systematic bias in one region of parameter space.** This often marks a region where the likelihood is flat in some direction.
3. **Correlations between recovered parameters that the true parameters do not have.** Rule 5 warns that fitting can *induce* a trade-off between parameters. An apparent relation between learning rate and inverse temperature across people may then be an artefact of estimation.

Recovery is a best case. The simulated data come from the very model being fitted, with no misspecification, no lapses and no fatigue. Good recovery is necessary before you interpret fitted parameters, but it is not sufficient. It also depends on the design and the parameter region together, and it can fail in one part of a region for reasons that do not apply in another, so check it across the whole region you intend to interpret.

## Model recovery

Rule 6 compares models and checks that the comparison works. The paper compares models with the Bayesian information criterion

$$\mathrm{BIC}=2\,\mathrm{NLL}(\hat\theta)+k\ln T,$$

where $k$ is the number of free parameters. Lower is better, and the $k\ln T$ term penalises flexibility. Equation 10 is printed as $-2\log\widehat{LL}+k_m\log T$, with $\widehat{LL}$ described as the log-likelihood at the best fit, which read literally takes the logarithm of a log-likelihood. The repository computes $k\ln T+2\,\mathrm{NLL}$, which is the form above.

Model recovery asks whether BIC picks the right model when the answer is known. Simulate many datasets from each model with parameters drawn from a stated region, fit every model to every dataset, and count which model wins. The result is the **confusion matrix** $P(\text{selected model}\mid\text{generating model})$, with one row per generating model. A matrix close to the identity says the comparison can tell the models apart *in this design and this parameter region*. Box 5 reports the matrix for all five bandit models, and a second version in which the softmax parameters are increased by 1, which shows how the matrix depends on the region.

The confusion matrix answers this question: if the data came from model $m$, how often would we select $m$? After fitting real data you face the reverse question: given that we selected $m$, how likely is it that $m$ generated the data? That is the **inversion matrix** $P(\text{generating model}\mid\text{selected model})$. By Bayes' rule it needs a prior over the generating models as well as the confusion matrix. The paper's Appendix 3 shows the computation.

## Comparing models on held-out data

BIC compares models on the data they were fitted to. The paper's examples compare and recover models in sample, mention cross-validation only as an alternative, and give no protocol for held-out evaluation. Huys et al. (2012) state the same limitation of their own analysis: their choice probabilities are predictive "but not in the machine learning sense". Dezfouli et al. (2019) show what a held-out protocol on real choice data looks like. They use leave-one-subject-out cross-validation, scoring each model on the choices of the subject left out by negative log-probability and the percentage of choices predicted.

A held-out number means nothing until you say what was held out from what. Every predictive result in this programme states its **information contract** first.

**Definition (information contract).** A predictive evaluation states, before any number:

1. **Observed:** what was observed.
2. **Prediction target:** what is predicted.
3. **History in training:** what history the model saw while it was fitted.
4. **History at inference:** what history is available when it predicts.
5. **Person:** whether the person is familiar (seen in training) or unseen.
6. **Task:** whether the task is familiar or unseen.
7. **Later time periods:** whether the evaluation covers periods later than training.
8. **Unavailable in real use:** anything used that would not be available in real use.
9. **Simplest baseline:** the baseline compared against.
10. **Claims not tested:** which claims the evaluation does not test.

The laboratory offers three splits and shows each one's contract before its numbers:

- **Later trials of the same participants.** Each participant's first 70% of trials are used for fitting and the remaining 30% are predicted. The person and the task are familiar, and the predicted trials are later.
- **Participants left out of the fit.** One participant is left out, each model is fitted to all the others with a single parameter set, and every choice of the left-out participant is predicted. The person is unseen and the task familiar.
- **Random trials.** About 30% of each participant's trials, chosen at random, are predicted from a fit to the rest. This split *leaks*. Trials that come after a held-out trial are in the training set, so the fitted parameters already reflect later behaviour, which no real forecast has. The laboratory includes it to make the leak visible in the contract, not as evidence of generalisation; the numbers alone need not show it.

Report held-out performance as the mean log-likelihood per held-out trial, with two reference points. One is the baseline model fitted on the same training data. The other is chance, $\ln\frac12\approx-0.693$ per trial with two arms. A model that beats chance but not the baseline has learned that the participant prefers one arm, not how they learn.

## When the data cannot decide: the ambiguous case

The ambiguous case changes three things at once. The session is short, 60 trials. The arms are close, 0.4 and 0.6. And the synthetic participants come from another region of parameter space, in which choices depend only weakly on learned values ($\beta$ at most 1.5) and win-stay lose-shift is followed only loosely ($\varepsilon$ from 0.6 to 0.95). Before you run it, predict what each of the three changes will do to parameter recovery and to the confusion matrix, and which of them matters most. The laboratory lets you change the number of trials, the reward probabilities and the participants' region one at a time, so each prediction can be tested separately.

Whatever you find, report an ambiguous result with the same discipline:

- say whether the evidence decides, and if it does not, report that as the finding;
- explain why, in terms of what the models predict on each trial;
- do not interpret any individual's fitted parameters where recovery is poor;
- propose a study that would decide, predict what it would do to the confusion matrix, and only then run it.

The sources add two cautions:

- **Recovery within one task does not make a parameter a stable property of the person.** Eckstein et al. (2022) studied 291 participants who each did three learning tasks. Absolute parameter values differed between tasks, and only some parameters partly generalised, and only between similar tasks.
- **A recoverable parameter can still be a poor measure of individual differences.** Unit 3 takes this up.

## Validating the winning model

Rule 8 says that whichever model wins, you should simulate it with its fitted parameters and check that the simulated behaviour reproduces the model-independent measures of the data. The rule distinguishes two uses of a fitted model:

- predicting each choice from the participant's actual history, which is what the likelihood scores;
- *free simulation*, in which the model generates its own history.

A model can predict the next choice well and still, when run freely, produce behaviour unlike the data. Box 7 shows a validation that fails because the fitted model performs too well. In the laboratory, *Validate the winning model by simulation* simulates the model with the lowest BIC twenty times with its fitted parameters and sets its model-independent measures beside the participant's. Run it once for the winning model of the informative case; unit 2 takes validation further.

## What this unit does not show

Everything in this unit is synthetic. The laboratory's participants are generated by one of the candidate models, so the true model is always among those compared, and real data carry no such guarantee. No result here says how real people learn, how they would behave on another task, or whether a fitted parameter would be the same next month. Those are the questions of units 3 to 8, and each needs its own contract.
