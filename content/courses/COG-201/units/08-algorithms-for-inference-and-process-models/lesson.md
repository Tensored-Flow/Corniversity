## Outline: Algorithms for inference and rational process models

This unit is **outlined, not yet prepared**. It will be written from the MCMC section of OCW 9.66J lecture 11 (the oct_14_2004_fin.pdf deck; Gibbs sampling and Metropolis–Hastings), lecture 10's sampling slides, and the web book's *Interlude - Algorithms for inference* and *Rational process models* chapters (reference-only).

**Scope.** Everything so far has been computational-level: posteriors defined, not computed. This unit asks how they could be computed, and whether the answer could be a theory of the mind at Marr's second level. Exact enumeration and why it fails; rejection sampling and its cost; Markov chain Monte Carlo with Gibbs sampling and Metropolis–Hastings, including detailed balance and the proposal distribution; particle filters for sequential data; variational inference as optimisation. Then the web book's rational process models: the sampling hypothesis (people's judgements look like a few samples from the posterior), its signatures (variability, probability matching, anchoring-like effects), and resource-rational analysis as the principled way to ask which approximation a bounded agent should use. The department convention is central here: an algorithm that reproduces behaviour is a candidate process model, and the evidence that distinguishes candidates is process-level (response times, trial-by-trial dynamics), not fit alone.

**What preparation will add.** A full lesson with detailed balance proved for Metropolis–Hastings, original exercises (a hand-run Gibbs sampler on a tiny model; a sample-complexity argument for how few samples a decision needs; a design that distinguishes "few samples" from "noisy exact inference"), instructor reference reasoning, an exit check and the unit's common misconceptions ("if people are Bayesian they must compute posteriors exactly"). Until then the reading above is accurate and the tutor can discuss the material, but there is no assessed work for this unit.
