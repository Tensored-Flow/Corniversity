## Grading notes (instructor only)

This is a seminar contribution; grade the argument, not the prose, and keep feedback under 150 words.

- The riddle: "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue" (green if observed before $t$, otherwise blue) are both confirmed by every emerald observed so far, yet we project only the first. The symmetry argument must appear: in a vocabulary with primitives grue and bleen, *green* is "grue if observed before $t$, otherwise bleen", so the time clause moves to the other hypothesis and syntactic simplicity, counted in primitives, cannot decide. A submission that says "grue is more complex" without the redescription has not met the first criterion.
- Entrenchment (lecture 1, slide 70): predicates entrenched through a history of successful projection are projectible. The lecture's two sources are prior experience (simple statistics of past success) and a causal theory; its preference is "theory supported by experience seems best". Both sources and the preference are expected.
- Examples: generalising that a novel animal's diet, not its location in the room, extends to its kind; projecting shape rather than colour when learning an object name; assuming a material's conductivity is lawlike while a room's occupancy by third sons is accidental. Any example where the competing predicates differ in projectibility rather than in truth is acceptable. The level question: an account in terms of entrenchment or theory describes what knowledge the learner brings to the inductive problem, which is level 1; a learner who argues that *acquiring* entrenchment is a level-2 story about process is making a defensible point and should be credited.
- Part 4: the hypothesis space and prior encode projectibility; "where the prior comes from" is the Bayesian form of Goodman's question. One sentence suffices.
- Watch for the misconception that simplicity settles the matter; address it with the redescription, not with authority.
