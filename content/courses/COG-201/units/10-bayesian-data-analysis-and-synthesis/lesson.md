## Outline: Bayesian data analysis and the evidence discipline; synthesis project

This unit is **outlined, not yet prepared**. It will be written from the web book's *Interlude - Bayesian data analysis* chapter (reference-only) and from the OCW course's projects page, which describes the final project or paper on cognitive modelling that this course's assessment a3 mirrors.

**Scope.** The chapter's distinction between people's models of coins and scientists' models of people is the department convention made formal. A cognitive model has parameters; behavioural data are evidence about them; posterior predictive checks say whether the model could have produced the data; Bayes factors (and the Savage–Dickey method for nested models) compare models; and Bayesian data analysis of cognitive models closes the loop between the computational-level theories of units 2–9 and the measurements they are supposed to explain. The unit returns to every earlier fit (the number game, the coin judgements) and asks: what was measured, how was the model fitted, how flexible was it, and what alternative would have fitted as well? The second half is the synthesis project: implement a Bayesian model of one phenomenon on synthetic data (labelled synthetic) or openly available behavioural data, compare it with a baseline model, and write up what the fit does and does not show.

**What preparation will add.** A full lesson, the project brief with its rubric unpacked, original exercises (a posterior predictive check computed by hand on a small data set; a Savage–Dickey Bayes factor for the Beta–Bernoulli coin; an audit of a described model–data comparison using the four-way labelling), instructor reference reasoning, an exit check and the unit's common misconceptions ("a flexible model that fits is better than a simple one that nearly fits"). Until then the reading above is accurate and the tutor can discuss the material, but there is no assessed work for this unit.
