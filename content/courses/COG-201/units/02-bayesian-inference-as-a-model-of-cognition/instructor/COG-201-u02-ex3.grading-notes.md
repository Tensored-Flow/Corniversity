## Grading notes (instructor only)

This is an explain-back; grade the ideas, not the prose, and keep feedback under 150 words.

- Model behaviour: after $\{16\}$ several hypotheses (even numbers, powers of two, perhaps square numbers or an interval near 16 in the full space) survive, so the profile is graded; after $\{16,8,2,64\}$ the powers of two dominate exponentially and the profile is all-or-none. Both halves are required.
- Measurement: the public slides say that generalisation judgements were collected from twenty participants ("N = 20" on the slide following the task slide) for the example sets listed on slide 43; the plots themselves are removed. A learner who quotes numerical ratings has invented them: mark `measurement` partially met and say why. A learner who says "the slides do not contain the data values" has understood the exercise.
- Evidence and level: a match is level-1 evidence that human generalisation is well described as Bayesian inference over this kind of hypothesis space; it is not evidence that people enumerate hypotheses, compute likelihoods or store posteriors. "Supports the model" is acceptable; "shows people are Bayesian" is the misconception the department convention targets, and should be corrected explicitly.
- Alternative models: similarity/exemplar models (graded for both sets, so they fail to produce the rule), rule-only models (all-or-none for both, so they fail to produce the gradient), or a distance heuristic. The discriminating experiment uses example sets on which the models disagree, for instance $\{16,8,2,64\}$ versus $\{16,23,19,20\}$ as on slide 43, or sets designed so that the smallest consistent hypothesis is unnatural.
- If the learner raises the point that the hypothesis space itself was chosen to fit the phenomena, credit it: it is a legitimate concern about the evidential value of the fit and anticipates unit 10.
