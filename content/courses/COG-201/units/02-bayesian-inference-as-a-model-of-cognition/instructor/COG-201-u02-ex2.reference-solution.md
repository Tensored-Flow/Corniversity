## Reference reasoning (instructor only)

**1. Size principle.** Strong sampling: the $n$ examples are drawn independently and uniformly at random from the true extension $h$ of the concept. Under $h$, a single example equals a particular number $x$ with probability $1/|h|$ if $x\in h$ and $0$ otherwise. By independence, $p(X\mid h)=\prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i\mid h)=|h|^{-n}$ if every $x_i\in h$, and $0$ if any example lies outside $h$. (With a uniform prior the posterior is the normalised likelihood, so the smallest consistent hypothesis is favoured by a factor that grows exponentially in $n$.)

**2. $X=\{60\}$.** $60$ is even, a multiple of 10, and in 50–60; it is not a power of two, so $h_3$ has likelihood $0$. Likelihoods: $h_1$: $1/50$; $h_2$: $1/10$; $h_4$: $1/11$. Sum $=\tfrac1{50}+\tfrac1{10}+\tfrac1{11}=\tfrac{11+55+50}{550}=\tfrac{116}{550}$. Posteriors: $p(h_1\mid X)=\tfrac{11}{116}=0.0948$; $p(h_2\mid X)=\tfrac{55}{116}=0.4741$; $p(h_4\mid X)=\tfrac{50}{116}=0.4310$. Generalisation by hypothesis averaging: $70\in h_1,h_2$, not $h_4$: $P(70\in C\mid X)=\tfrac{66}{116}=0.569$; $52\in h_1,h_4$: $P(52\in C\mid X)=\tfrac{61}{116}=0.526$; $55\in h_4$ only: $P(55\in C\mid X)=\tfrac{50}{116}=0.431$. A graded profile: numbers near 60, even numbers and multiples of ten all receive substantial probability.

**3. $X=\{60,80,10,30\}$.** $80\notin h_4$ and none are powers of two, so only $h_1$ and $h_2$ survive. Likelihoods $(1/50)^4=1.6\times10^{-7}$ and $(1/10)^4=10^{-4}$; sum $1.0016\times10^{-4}$. Posteriors: $p(h_2\mid X)=\tfrac{10^{-4}}{1.0016\times10^{-4}}=0.99840$, $p(h_1\mid X)=0.00160$ (exactly $625/626$ and $1/626$). Generalisation: $P(70\in C\mid X)=1$; $P(52\in C\mid X)=0.0016$; $P(55\in C\mid X)=0$. A rule: multiples of ten, all or nothing.

**4. Graded versus rule-like.** After one example three hypotheses survive with posteriors of the same order ($0.09$, $0.47$, $0.43$), so averaging mixes their extensions and every number in any of them gets intermediate probability. After four examples the likelihood ratio between the two survivors is $(50/10)^4=625$, so the smaller hypothesis takes essentially all the mass and averaging reproduces its extension exactly. Four examples would fail to produce a rule only if the hypothesis space contained several consistent hypotheses of similar size (for instance "multiples of 10" and "multiples of 10 or 5", sizes 10 and 20, give a ratio of only $2^4=16$), or if the prior strongly favoured a larger hypothesis.

**5. Likelihood and prior.** The size principle makes the likelihood prefer smaller consistent extensions, so a prior that penalised complexity would be redundant for that purpose: the book's "does not have to penalize complexity" refers to this. But the likelihood would equally favour tiny gerrymandered hypotheses ("multiples of 10 except 50 and 70", size 8, beats "multiples of 10" on any consistent data), so the prior, and before it the choice of which hypotheses exist at all, must assign such hypotheses negligible weight: the lecture's "embodies a strong prior" refers to this. Division of labour: likelihood handles fit and implicit negative evidence; prior and hypothesis space handle naturalness.

**Alternative valid approaches.** Writing posteriors as exact fractions over $116$ and $626$; computing generalisation by first listing survivors containing each test number; a log-space argument for part 4. Accept a learner who notes that with weak sampling (examples chosen without regard to the concept) the size principle disappears and the four examples would only exclude hypotheses, not favour the smaller one; that is the correct contrast and worth a comment.

**Common gaps.** Forgetting $h_4$ for $X=\{60\}$ (gives $1/6$ and $5/6$); counting the interval 50–60 as 10 numbers; keeping $h_4$ alive after $80$; computing $P(52)$ without $h_4$; describing the size principle as a prior.
