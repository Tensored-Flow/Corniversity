## Reference reasoning (instructor only)

**1. Transformation law.** On $U\cap V$ the coordinate functions $x^i$ are smooth functions of $(y^1,\dots,y^n)$ through the transition map $x\circ y^{-1}$. For a smooth function $f$, the chain rule gives $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y^a}=\dfrac{\partial x^i}{\partial y^a}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x^i}$; as derivations this is $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y^a}=\dfrac{\partial x^i}{\partial y^a}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x^i}$. Bilinearity of $g$ then gives
$$\tilde g_{ab}=g\Big(\frac{\partial x^i}{\partial y^a}\partial_i,\frac{\partial x^j}{\partial y^b}\partial_j\Big)=\frac{\partial x^i}{\partial y^a}\frac{\partial x^j}{\partial y^b}\,g(\partial_i,\partial_j)=\frac{\partial x^i}{\partial y^a}\frac{\partial x^j}{\partial y^b}\,g_{ij}.$$
In matrix form, with $J^i{}_a=\partial x^i/\partial y^a$, this is $\tilde G=J^tGJ$.

**2. Determinant and positivity.** $\det\tilde G=\det(J^t)\det G\det J=(\det J)^2\det G$. For $v\in\mathbb{R}^n\setminus\{0\}$, $v^t\tilde Gv=(Jv)^tG(Jv)$, and $Jv\neq0$ because $J$ is invertible (the transition map is a diffeomorphism); so $\tilde G$ is positive definite iff $G$ is. Hence "$(g_{ij})(p)$ is positive definite" is a property of $g_p$, not of the chart.

**3. Polar coordinates.** $P$ is smooth, injective on $(0,\infty)\times(0,2\pi)$ (the modulus determines $r$, then the angle in $(0,2\pi)$ is determined), and its image is the slit plane $W=\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{(x,0):x\ge0\}$, which is open. Its Jacobian
$$J=\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,\theta)}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det J=r>0,$$
is invertible on the domain, so by the inverse function theorem $P$ is a diffeomorphism onto $W$ and $P^{-1}$ is a chart (explicitly $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ and $\theta$ the angle function, continuous on the slit plane). The Euclidean metric has $G=I$ in Cartesian coordinates, so by part 1, $\tilde G=J^tJ$:
$$g_{rr}=\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1,\qquad g_{r\theta}=-r\cos\theta\sin\theta+r\sin\theta\cos\theta=0,\qquad g_{\theta\theta}=r^2\sin^2\theta+r^2\cos^2\theta=r^2,$$
so $g=dr^2+r^2d\theta^2$ and $\det(g_{ij})=r^2$. By part 2, $\det\tilde G=(\det J)^2\det G=r^2\cdot1$: the factor that tends to zero as $r\to0$ is $(\det J)^2$, the Jacobian of the parametrisation, which fails to be a local diffeomorphism along $r=0$ (the whole segment $\{0\}\times(0,2\pi)$ would map to the origin). The factor $\det G=1$ does not degenerate: in the Cartesian chart, which does contain the origin, $g$ is the identity matrix there. The singularity is in the coordinates, not in the metric.

**4. Density.** By part 2, $\sqrt{\det\tilde G}=|\det J|\sqrt{\det G}$, so $\sqrt{\det(g_{ij})}$ is not a function on $M$ (a function would be unchanged); it is a *density*, and $\sqrt{\det(g_{ij})}\,dx^1\cdots dx^n$ is the Riemannian volume element (on an oriented manifold, the volume form $\sqrt{\det g}\,dx^1\wedge\dots\wedge dx^n$), because the Jacobian factor is exactly the change-of-variables factor for integrals. Statement only; the proof belongs to integration on manifolds.

**Alternative valid approaches.** Part 1 via the pull-back of the tensor $g_{ij}dx^i\otimes dx^j$ with $dx^i=(\partial x^i/\partial y^a)dy^a$; part 3 via the Gram criterion ($\partial_rP$ and $\partial_\theta P$ are orthogonal of lengths $1$ and $r$). Accept.

**Common gaps.** Index placement errors ($J$ versus $J^{-1}$); forgetting $\det J^t=\det J$; asserting the metric "is singular at the origin"; calling $\det g$ a scalar; omitting the slit when describing the chart domain.
