## Reference reasoning (instructor only)

**Conventions.** Signature $(+,+)$; on the slit plane $x^1=r$, $x^2=\theta$; $g_{ij}=g(\partial_i,\partial_j)$ with lower indices; $E^2=(\mathbb{R}^2,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)$.

**1. Determinants.** $(g_{ij})=\mathrm{diag}(1,\alpha^2r^2)$, so $\det(g_{ij})=\alpha^2r^2$; for $g_1$ it is $r^2$. Both tend to $0$ as $r\to0$, at the same rate up to the constant factor $\alpha^2$. By the Gram criterion and the determinant law of exercise 2 this reports that the polar chart degenerates at $r=0$ (which is not even in its domain). It reports exactly the same for both metrics, so by itself it cannot say whether some other chart removes the degeneracy. For $g_1$ one does: the Cartesian chart, in which $g_1$ is the identity matrix (exercise 2).

**2. Locally Euclidean away from the origin.** $\partial_r\Psi=(\cos\alpha\theta,\sin\alpha\theta)$ and $\partial_\theta\Psi=\alpha r(-\sin\alpha\theta,\cos\alpha\theta)$, so the Euclidean inner products are $1$, $0$ and $\alpha^2r^2$: the pull-back of the Euclidean metric by $\Psi$ is $dr^2+\alpha^2r^2d\theta^2$, which is $g_\alpha$. Since $0<\alpha\theta<2\pi\alpha<2\pi$, the pair $(r,\alpha\theta)$ is the polar representation of $\Psi(r,\theta)$ and determines $(r,\theta)$, so $\Psi$ is injective; its image is the open sector $S_\alpha=\{(\rho\cos\phi,\rho\sin\phi):\rho>0,\ 0<\phi<2\pi\alpha\}$. The Jacobian determinant of $\Psi$ is $\alpha r>0$, so by the inverse function theorem $\Psi$ is a diffeomorphism onto $S_\alpha$, and $\Psi\circ P^{-1}$ is an isometry from the slit plane with $g_\alpha$ onto $S_\alpha\subseteq E^2$. Every point of $M$ lies in a slit plane whose slit avoids it; in that polar chart $g_\alpha$ has the same form, and the same map gives an isometry onto a sector. So $(M,g_\alpha)$ is locally isometric to $E^2$ at every point: away from the origin no local computation tells $g_\alpha$ from $g_1$.

**3. Cartesian coefficients.** On $M$, $dr=(x\,dx+y\,dy)/r$ and $r\,d\theta=(x\,dy-y\,dx)/r$, the latter being the same 1-form in every polar chart. Hence
$$g_\alpha=\frac{x^2+\alpha^2y^2}{x^2+y^2}\,dx^2+2\,\frac{(1-\alpha^2)xy}{x^2+y^2}\,dx\,dy+\frac{\alpha^2x^2+y^2}{x^2+y^2}\,dy^2 .$$
The coefficients are bounded: $g_{xx},g_{yy}\in[\alpha^2,1]$, and $|g_{xy}|\le\tfrac12(1-\alpha^2)$ because $|xy|\le\tfrac12(x^2+y^2)$. On the $x$-axis $g_{xx}=1$ and on the $y$-axis $g_{xx}=\alpha^2$; as $\alpha\neq1$, $g_{xx}$ has no limit at the origin, so $g_\alpha$ has no continuous extension in this chart. That settles the Cartesian chart only. For $g_1$ it was exactly a change of chart that removed the degeneracy, so other charts have to be excluded by an invariant, which is what parts 4 and 5 provide.

**4. Distance from the origin.** For $v=v^r\partial_r+v^\theta\partial_\theta$, $g_\alpha(v,v)=(v^r)^2+\alpha^2r^2(v^\theta)^2\ge(v^r)^2=(dr(v))^2$. The function $r$ is smooth on all of $M$, so the inequality holds at every point, across any slit. For a piecewise $C^1$ curve $\gamma:[a,b]\to M$,
$$L(\gamma)=\int_a^b\sqrt{g_\alpha(\dot\gamma,\dot\gamma)}\,dt\ \ge\ \int_a^b\Big|\frac{d}{dt}\,r(\gamma(t))\Big|\,dt\ \ge\ \big|r(\gamma(b))-r(\gamma(a))\big| .$$
A radial segment $t\mapsto(t,\theta_0)$ for $t$ between $r_1$ and $r_2$ has speed $1$ and length $|r_2-r_1|$, so the bound is attained. The circle $r=\varepsilon$, parametrised by $\theta$, has speed $\sqrt{g_{\theta\theta}}=\alpha\varepsilon$ and length $2\pi\alpha\varepsilon$ (the single point on the slit does not affect the length).

**5. No chart removes the singularity.** Assume $(U,\varphi)$ and $h$ as stated. Replacing $U$ by its connected component containing $0$ (still open, and still a neighbourhood of $0$), $(U,h)$ is a connected Riemannian surface with the smooth structure given by $\varphi$; it coincides with $(U\setminus\{0\},g_\alpha)$ away from $0$, and its topology is the usual one because $\varphi$ is a homeomorphism. Let $d_h$ be its distance; Proposition 5.10 applies to it.

*Lower bound.* Let $q\in U\setminus\{0\}$ and let $\gamma:[a,b]\to U$ be piecewise $C^1$ from $0$ to $q$. The set $\gamma^{-1}(0)$ is closed and non-empty, so it has a largest element $t_0<b$. For $t_0<s\le b$ the restriction of $\gamma$ to $[s,b]$ lies in $U\setminus\{0\}$, where $h=g_\alpha$ and the two smooth structures agree, so by part 4 its length is at least $r(q)-r(\gamma(s))$. As $s$ decreases to $t_0$, $r(\gamma(s))\to r(0)=0$ by continuity. Hence $L(\gamma)\ge r(q)$, and $d_h(0,q)\ge r(q)$.

*Upper bound.* Choose $\varepsilon_0>0$ with the closed disc $\{r\le\varepsilon_0\}$ inside $U$. For $q$ with $r(q)\le\varepsilon_0$ and $0<\delta<r(q)$, let $q_\delta$ be the point at radius $\delta$ on the ray through $q$. The radial segment from $q_\delta$ to $q$ lies in $U\setminus\{0\}$ and has length $r(q)-\delta$, so $d_h(0,q)\le d_h(0,q_\delta)+r(q)-\delta$. As $\delta\to0$, $q_\delta\to0$ in the topology of $U$, and $d_h$ induces that topology (Proposition 5.10), so $d_h(0,q_\delta)\to0$. Hence $d_h(0,q)\le r(q)$.

*The distance circle.* So $d_h(0,q)=r(q)$ when $r(q)\le\varepsilon_0$, and $d_h(0,q)\ge r(q)$ for every $q\in U$. For $0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_0$, points with $r=\varepsilon$ are at distance $\varepsilon$, points with $r<\varepsilon$ are at distance $r<\varepsilon$, and points of $U$ with $r>\varepsilon$ are at distance greater than $\varepsilon$. The set at $h$-distance exactly $\varepsilon$ from $0$ is therefore the circle $r=\varepsilon$, whose $h$-length equals its $g_\alpha$-length $2\pi\alpha\varepsilon$. By (F), $L(\varepsilon)/\varepsilon=2\pi\alpha$ would have to tend to $2\pi$, forcing $\alpha=1$, which contradicts $\alpha\in(0,1)$. No such chart exists.

**6. Coordinates and geometry.** *Coordinates:* for both metrics the polar chart stops being a chart at $r=0$ and the determinant tends to $0$ (part 1), and the Cartesian chart is the obvious candidate at the origin; it removes the degeneracy of $g_1$ and fails for $g_\alpha$ (part 3), but the failure of one chart proves nothing about every chart. *Geometry:* the length of the circle of points at distance $\varepsilon$ from the origin, divided by $\varepsilon$, is built from lengths and distances alone, so it does not depend on any chart and is preserved by isometries. It is $2\pi$ for $g_1$, as (F) requires of every smooth metric, and $2\pi\alpha$ for $g_\alpha$: the total angle around the origin is $2\pi\alpha$, a cone point. That is a property of the space $(M,g_\alpha)$ with the origin added, not of the polar chart. Parts 1 and 2 cannot separate the cases (the same determinant behaviour; both locally Euclidean away from $0$), part 3 is suggestive only, and parts 4 and 5 decide.

**Alternative valid approaches.** Part 2 by the substitution $\phi=\alpha\theta$, which turns $g_\alpha$ into $d\rho^2+\rho^2d\phi^2$ on a sector, so the cone is a sector of angle $2\pi\alpha$ with its edges glued. Part 3 via $g_\alpha=g_1-(1-\alpha^2)(x\,dy-y\,dx)^2/(x^2+y^2)$. In part 5, any correct argument by an isometry invariant that uses (F), or that proves an equivalent statement about small distance circles, is acceptable; for example comparing the distance between diametrically opposite points of the circle $r=\varepsilon$ with $2\varepsilon$, but only if the matching smooth-surface fact (small distance circles have diameter $2\varepsilon$) is stated as an assumption alongside (F).

**Common gaps.** Concluding from part 1 that $g_\alpha$ has a coordinate singularity like the polar one; treating part 3 as a proof that no chart works; in part 4, proving the inequality only inside one polar chart without noting that $r$ is a global function; in part 5, asserting $d_h(0,q)=r(q)$ without both inequalities, or forgetting that the curves in the lower bound may pass through $0$ more than once; calling the cone point a singularity of the metric away from the origin (it is locally Euclidean there; everything is concentrated at the point).
