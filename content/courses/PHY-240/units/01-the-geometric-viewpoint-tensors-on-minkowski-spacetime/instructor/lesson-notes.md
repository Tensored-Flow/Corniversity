## Lesson notes (instructor only)

Source mapping, MIT 8.962 Spring 2020 (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0). Lecture Guide (MIT8_962S20_guide.pdf, printed page = index + 1): Lecture 1 — Lorentz transformations and invariant intervals, displacement 4-vector, definition of 4-vector by transformation law, component notation, basis vectors, inner product and metric (p. 1); Lecture 2 — coordinate bases, 4-velocity, 4-momentum, 4-acceleration, $(M,N)$ tensors, raising and lowering, derivatives in rectilinear coordinates, number flux and conservation (p. 1); Lecture 3 — contraction, dual nature of vectors and 1-forms (p. 1). Video pages for Lectures 1–3 carry transcripts. Tong, *General Relativity*, Section 1.2.1 (reference-only, signature $(-+++)$) treats the relativistic particle action; Carroll Chapter 1 (reference-only) is the fuller textbook account. The lesson's statement about the (1,1) versus (2,0) trace is the build agent's own clarification, not the Guide's.

Teaching intent: the learner is a mathematician, so the multilinear-map definition will be welcome and the index gymnastics will feel like bookkeeping; the physics to insist on is the *meaning* of components ($N^0$ is what an observer calls density), the causal character of $u$ and $a$, and the discipline of naming the transformation class behind every "is a tensor" (exercise 2's automatic check enforces this). The hyperbolic worldline is worth connecting forward: it is the Rindler observer whose horizon reappears, in curved form, at the Schwarzschild horizon in unit 11.

Watch for: inner products without $\eta$; $a$ declared timelike; $c$ restored by guesswork rather than dimensional analysis; "the metric is invariant" stated without "under Lorentz transformations".

Staged misconception for a peer discussion, if peers are enabled: a classmate may argue that "$\partial_\mu A_\nu$ is a tensor because it has two indices". Resolve through the second-derivative term in exercise 2, then point to unit 2 for the repair.
