## Lesson notes (instructor only)

Source mapping, MIT 8.962 Spring 2020 Lecture Guide (printed page = index + 1), PDF page 1: Lecture 4 — volume elements via the Levi-Civita tensor (footnote: a tensor in special relativity formulated in inertial coordinates), differential and integral conservation laws, electrodynamics in geometric language (4-current, Faraday tensor), the stress-energy tensor with the perfect fluid as the key example; Lecture 5 — symmetry and physical meaning of $T$'s components, differential conservation of energy and momentum, "prelude to curvature": tensor analysis in curvilinear coordinates, the remark that $\mathbf e_\phi$ must have dimensions of length, the Christoffel symbol as "the quantity (not a tensor!) which relates derivatives of basis objects to basis objects", the covariant derivative, and $\nabla g = 0$ motivated by the principle of equivalence. Video pages for Lectures 4–5 carry transcripts. Tong, *General Relativity*, Chapter 2 and Carroll Chapters 1 and 3 (reference-only) are the fuller accounts; the lesson's angular-momentum argument for the symmetry of $T^{ij}$ and the explicit polar-coordinate computations are the build agent's own standard presentations, not quotations.

Two deliberate choices: Maxwell's equations are written with an unspecified constant because unit conventions (SI, Gaussian, Heaviside–Lorentz) differ and the source was not checked for its convention; and the exercise asks for the dust geodesic argument with $\partial$ rather than $\nabla$, so that the learner meets $u^\mu\partial_\mu u^\nu = 0$ in flat space before the covariant version in unit 4.

Teaching intent: the learner is comfortable with manifolds, so the risk is boredom with polar coordinates rather than confusion; counter it by insisting on the physics of $T^{\mu\nu}$ (meaning of each component, the two approximations in the Newtonian limit, where they fail in nature). The spherical-coordinate exercise is long but worth doing once by hand; after this unit, Christoffel symbols may be computed by any means.

Watch for: $\eta^{\mu\nu}$ sign errors in the perfect fluid; "Newtonian limit" without hypotheses; mixing orthonormal and coordinate components; claiming $\Gamma\neq0$ means curvature.

Staged misconception for a peer discussion, if peers are enabled: a classmate may say "pressure is just the diagonal of $T^{ij}$, so it cannot gravitate". Resolve through $(\rho + P)u^\mu u^\nu$ and point forward to the $\rho + 3P$ in the Friedmann acceleration equation (unit 9).
