## Reference reasoning (instructor only)

**1. Decreasing monotone convergence under $\int f_1<\infty$.** Put $g_n=f_1-f_n$. Because the $f_n$ are finite-valued the difference is defined everywhere; $g_n\ge0$ since $f_n\le f_1$; $g_n$ is measurable as a difference of real measurable functions (unit 1, Theorem 0.2 with $\Phi(s,t)=s-t$); $g_n\le g_{n+1}$ since $f_{n+1}\le f_n$; and $g_n\to f_1-f$ pointwise, where $f=\lim f_n$ is finite because $0\le f\le f_1<\infty$. The monotone convergence theorem gives $\int g_n\to\int(f_1-f)$.

Now $f_1=g_n+f_n$ with both summands nonnegative, so additivity (lecture 4, Theorem 0.3) gives $\int f_1=\int g_n+\int f_n$; similarly $f_1=(f_1-f)+f$ gives $\int f_1=\int(f_1-f)+\int f$. Because $\int f_1<\infty$, all four integrals $\int g_n,\int f_n,\int(f_1-f),\int f$ are finite (each is at most $\int f_1$ by monotonicity), so we may subtract: $\int g_n=\int f_1-\int f_n$ and $\int(f_1-f)=\int f_1-\int f$. Hence $\int f_1-\int f_n\to\int f_1-\int f$, i.e. $\int f_n\to\int f$. The finiteness hypothesis is used exactly at the subtraction (an equation $\infty=\infty+a$ determines nothing about $a$). $\square$

Remark for the assessor: if only $\int f_1<\infty$ is assumed and $f_1$ may take the value $\infty$, then $f_1<\infty$ almost everywhere and the proof goes through after redefining everything on a null set; that is unit 3 material, which is why the prompt assumes finite values.

**2. Counterexample.** On $\mathbb N$ with counting measure let $f_n=\chi_{\{n,n+1,\dots\}}$. Then $f_1\ge f_2\ge\cdots$, $f_n(k)=0$ for $n>k$ so $f_n\to0$ pointwise, but $\int f_n\,d\mu=\mu(\{n,n+1,\dots\})=\infty$ for every $n$, while $\int0=0$. (Also valid: $f_n\equiv\frac1n$ on $\mathbb N$, integral $\infty$, limit $0$.)

**3. The computation.** $f_n(k)=2^{-k}(1+k/n)$. Hypotheses: $f_n\ge0$ and finite; for fixed $k$, $k/n$ decreases in $n$, so $f_n(k)\ge f_{n+1}(k)$; $f_n(k)\to2^{-k}=:f(k)$. Using exercise 1 and $\sum_{k\ge1}2^{-k}=1$, $\sum_{k\ge1}k2^{-k}=\frac{1/2}{(1-1/2)^2}=2$:
$$\int f_1\,d\mu=\sum_k2^{-k}(1+k)=1+2=3<\infty,$$
$$\int f_n\,d\mu=\sum_k2^{-k}+\frac1n\sum_kk2^{-k}=1+\frac2n\longrightarrow1,\qquad\int f\,d\mu=\sum_k2^{-k}=1 .$$
So $\lim\int f_n=\int f$, as part 1 predicts.

**Alternative valid approaches.** Part 1 can be done by applying Fatou to $g_n$ and to $f_n$ separately; or by dominated convergence once it is available (unit 3), which the learner should not use yet; accept with a note. For the sums, a learner may differentiate the geometric series or use $\sum_{k\ge1}k2^{-k}=\sum_{j\ge1}\sum_{k\ge j}2^{-k}=\sum_j2^{-j+1}=2$, which nicely uses part 2 of exercise 1.

**Common gaps.** Applying the monotone convergence theorem directly to the decreasing $f_n$; not noticing that $f=\lim f_n$ must be finite for $f_1-f$ to make sense; asserting "$\int g_n=\int f_1-\int f_n$" without saying the integrals are finite; arithmetic slips giving $\int f_1=2$ or $\int f_n=1+1/n$.
