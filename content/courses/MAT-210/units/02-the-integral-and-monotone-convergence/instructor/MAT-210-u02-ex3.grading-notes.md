## Grading notes (instructor only)

Expected content, part (a): monotonicity is used (i) to make $\int f_n$ an increasing sequence of numbers, so that $\alpha=\lim\int f_n$ exists in $[0,\infty]$, and (ii) to make $E_n=\{f_n\ge cs\}$ an increasing sequence of sets, so that continuity from below of the measure $E\mapsto\int_Es\,d\mu$ gives $\int_{E_n}s\to\int_Xs$. The constant $c<1$ is needed for $\bigcup_nE_n=X$: at a point with $s(x)=f(x)>0$ the approximants may satisfy $f_n(x)<f(x)=s(x)$ for every $n$ (e.g. $f_n=(1-1/n)f$), so with $c=1$ that point would lie in no $E_n$; the slack $cs(x)<s(x)\le f(x)$ fixes this, and it also covers points where $f(x)=\infty$ and $s(x)$ is finite. Some learners add that nonnegativity is what makes $\int_{E_n}f_n\le\int_Xf_n$; fine.

Part (b): Fatou gives $\int\liminf f_n\le\liminf\int f_n$ for any sequence of nonnegative measurable functions, no monotonicity required, by applying the monotone convergence theorem to $g_k=\inf_{n\ge k}f_n$. Equality fails in general: $f_n=\chi_{\{n\}}$ on $\mathbb N$ gives $0\le1$. Any correct example counts (bumps of fixed mass escaping to infinity, or bumps concentrating: $f_n=n\chi_{\{1\}}\cdot\frac1n$ is not an example; check the learner's arithmetic).

Mark as a conceptual error any statement that Fatou holds for arbitrary (sign-changing) sequences without a lower bound, or that the inequality is reversed.

Keep feedback under 120 words.
