## Reference reasoning (instructor only)

**1. The σ-algebra of a finite partition.** Let $\mathcal A=\{\bigcup_{i\in S}A_i : S\subset\{1,\dots,n\}\}$ (with $S=\emptyset$ giving $\emptyset$). Claim: $\sigma(\{A_1,\dots,A_n\})=\mathcal A$.

$\mathcal A$ is a σ-algebra: $X=\bigcup_{i\le n}A_i\in\mathcal A$; the complement of $\bigcup_{i\in S}A_i$ is $\bigcup_{i\notin S}A_i$ because the blocks partition $X$; a countable union $\bigcup_k\bigcup_{i\in S_k}A_i$ equals $\bigcup_{i\in\bigcup_kS_k}A_i\in\mathcal A$. It contains each block (take $S=\{i\}$). So $\sigma(\{A_i\})\subset\mathcal A$. Conversely every member of $\mathcal A$ is a finite union of blocks, hence lies in any σ-algebra containing the blocks; so $\mathcal A\subset\sigma(\{A_i\})$.

Count: the map $S\mapsto\bigcup_{i\in S}A_i$ is injective because the blocks are nonempty and disjoint (if $S\ne S'$, pick $i$ in one but not the other and a point $x\in A_i$; $x$ lies in exactly one of the two unions). Hence $|\sigma(\{A_i\})|=2^n$. Without "nonempty" the count can drop: two empty blocks give the same union.

**2. The countable–co-countable σ-algebra.** (σ1) $X^c=\emptyset$ is countable. (σ2) The definition is symmetric in $A$ and $A^c$. (σ3) Let $A_i\in\mathcal M_c$. If every $A_i$ is countable, $\bigcup A_i$ is a countable union of countable sets, hence countable. Otherwise some $A_j$ has countable complement, and $(\bigcup_iA_i)^c=\bigcap_iA_i^c\subset A_j^c$ is countable. Either way $\bigcup A_i\in\mathcal M_c$.

Generated by singletons: each $\{x\}$ is countable, so $\{x\}\in\mathcal M_c$ and $\sigma(\{\{x\}\})\subset\mathcal M_c$. Conversely, a countable $A=\{x_1,x_2,\dots\}$ equals $\bigcup_k\{x_k\}\in\sigma(\{\{x\}\})$, and a co-countable $A$ is the complement of such a set. So $\mathcal M_c\subset\sigma(\{\{x\}\})$.

Uncountability of $X$ is not needed for part 2 (for countable $X$ both sides are $\mathcal P(X)$); it is what makes the example interesting and is essential in exercise 2.

**3. Finite–cofinite.** (σ1): $X^c=\emptyset$ finite. (σ2): symmetric. Finite unions: finite $\cup$ finite is finite; if one set is cofinite the union is cofinite (its complement is inside a finite set). Failure of (σ3): since $X$ is infinite it contains a countably infinite subset $\{x_1,x_2,\dots\}$ (distinct points). Each $\{x_{2k}\}\in\mathcal F$, but $E=\bigcup_k\{x_{2k}\}=\{x_2,x_4,\dots\}$ is infinite and $E^c\supset\{x_1,x_3,\dots\}$ is infinite, so $E\notin\mathcal F$. Axiom (σ3), closure under countable unions, fails.

**Alternative valid approaches.** Part 1 may be argued by induction on $n$ or by noting that the atoms of any σ-algebra containing the blocks are unions of blocks. Part 3: any infinite set with infinite complement built as a countable union of finite sets works (e.g. $\{x_k: k\text{ prime}\}$). Accept $X=\mathbb N$ with the even numbers if the learner first reduces to a countably infinite subset, or notes that the problem is unchanged by that reduction; if they treat only $X=\mathbb N$ without comment, mark the generality criterion partially met.

**Common gaps.** Forgetting that distinctness of the $2^n$ unions needs nonempty blocks; in part 2 asserting (σ3) "since countable unions of countable sets are countable" without the co-countable case; in part 3 choosing a sequence whose union is cofinite (e.g. all of $\{x_k\}$ when $X$ is countable) which is still in $\mathcal F$; naming the wrong axiom.
