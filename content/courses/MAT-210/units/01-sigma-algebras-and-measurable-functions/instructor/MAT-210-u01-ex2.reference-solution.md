## Reference reasoning (instructor only)

**1. Rational test values suffice.** If $f$ is measurable then $\{f>q\}=f^{-1}((q,\infty])$ is measurable for every $q$. Conversely suppose $\{f>q\}\in\mathcal M$ for all $q\in\mathbb Q$ and let $\alpha\in\mathbb R$. Then
$$\{f>\alpha\}=\bigcup_{q\in\mathbb Q,\ q>\alpha}\{f>q\}.$$
Indeed if $f(x)>\alpha$ (including $f(x)=\infty$) there is a rational $q$ with $\alpha<q<f(x)$, so $x$ lies in the right side; and if $f(x)>q>\alpha$ then $f(x)>\alpha$. The right side is a countable union of measurable sets, so $\{f>\alpha\}\in\mathcal M$ for every real $\alpha$, and $f$ is measurable by lecture 2, Proposition 0.1(c).

**2. Characterisation of $\mathcal M_c$-measurable functions.** Let $X$ be uncountable and $f:X\to\mathbb R$.

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $N=\{f\ne c\}$ is countable. For any $U\subset\mathbb R$ (open or not): if $c\in U$ then $f^{-1}(U)\supset X\setminus N$, so $f^{-1}(U)^c\subset N$ is countable; if $c\notin U$ then $f^{-1}(U)\subset N$ is countable. In both cases $f^{-1}(U)\in\mathcal M_c$.

($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $f$ is $\mathcal M_c$-measurable. For each rational $q$ the set $L_q=\{f\le q\}=f^{-1}((-\infty,q])$ is measurable (preimage of a closed, hence Borel, set), so it is countable or co-countable. Let $S=\{q\in\mathbb Q: L_q\text{ countable}\}$.

- $S$ is a down-set: if $q\in S$ and $q'<q$ then $L_{q'}\subset L_q$ is countable.
- $S\ne\mathbb Q$: otherwise $X=\bigcup_{q\in\mathbb Q}L_q$ (every real value is at most some rational) would be countable.
- $S\ne\emptyset$: otherwise every $L_q$ is co-countable, i.e. every $\{f>q\}$ is countable, and $X=\bigcup_{q\in\mathbb Q}\{f>q\}$ would be countable.

So $S$ is a nonempty proper down-set of $\mathbb Q$ and $c=\sup S\in\mathbb R$ (bounded above by any rational outside $S$). Now $\{f<c\}=\bigcup_{q\in\mathbb Q,\ q<c}L_q$, and each such $q$ lies in $S$ (there is $s\in S$ with $q<s$, and $S$ is a down-set), so $\{f<c\}$ is a countable union of countable sets, hence countable. And $\{f>c\}=\bigcup_{q\in\mathbb Q,\ q>c}\{f>q\}$; for $q>c$ we have $q\notin S$, so $L_q$ is co-countable and $\{f>q\}=L_q^c$ is countable; hence $\{f>c\}$ is countable. Therefore $\{f\ne c\}=\{f<c\}\cup\{f>c\}$ is countable.

Both uses of uncountability are essential: for countable $X$ every function is measurable and the characterisation fails.

**3. Limits directly.** Let $N_n=\{f_n\ne c_n\}$, countable, and $N=\bigcup_nN_n$, countable. Since $X$ is uncountable, $X\setminus N\ne\emptyset$; pick $x_0\in X\setminus N$. For every $x\in X\setminus N$, $f_n(x)=c_n$ for all $n$, so $c_n=f_n(x_0)\to f(x_0)=:c$: the sequence of constants converges, and $f(x)=\lim_nf_n(x)=\lim_nc_n=c$ for every $x\in X\setminus N$. Thus $\{f\ne c\}\subset N$ is countable and $f$ is $\mathcal M_c$-measurable by part 2, with constant $c=\lim_nc_n$. This agrees with Theorem 0.3 of lecture 2 and shows what the theorem means in this σ-algebra.

**Alternative valid approaches.** In part 2 ($\Rightarrow$), some learners argue with $\{f<q\}$ or $\{f\ge q\}$ instead of $\{f\le q\}$, or define $c=\inf\{q: L_q\text{ co-countable}\}$; all fine. Another route: the sets $\{f=t\}$, $t\in\mathbb R$, are disjoint and measurable; at most one can be co-countable (two disjoint co-countable sets would make $X$ countable), and if none is co-countable then all are countable; one then shows some $\{f=t\}$ must be co-countable because otherwise $X$ is a union of... this needs care (an uncountable union of countable sets can be uncountable), so a learner taking this route must still produce the sup argument or an equivalent; mark partially met if they stop at "at most one value is taken co-countably often" without proving that one is.

**Common gaps.** Omitting why $S$ is nonempty (needs $f$ real-valued and $X$ uncountable); concluding from "$L_q$ countable for $q<c$" that $\{f\le c\}$ is countable (false in general: $L_c$ may be co-countable; only $\{f<c\}$ is a union of the $L_q$ with $q<c$); in part 3 forgetting to justify that $(c_n)$ converges.
