## Why measure theory

The Riemann integral behaves badly under limits: the indicator of the rationals in $[0,1]$ is a pointwise limit of indicators of finite sets, each with Riemann integral $0$, yet it is not Riemann integrable at all. Lebesgue's idea reverses the order of business: first decide how to measure the *size* of sets, then integrate by slicing the range of the function rather than its domain. Lecture 1 opens exactly here ("we need to know how to measure the size of subsets of a space $X$ before we can integrate") and warns at once that a measure will not be defined on all subsets of $X$. This unit is about which collections of sets are eligible, and which functions can then be integrated.

## σ-algebras

**Definition (σ-algebra).** Let $X$ be a set. A collection $\mathcal M\subset\mathcal P(X)$ is a σ-algebra on $X$ if

1. (σ1) $X\in\mathcal M$;
2. (σ2) if $A\in\mathcal M$ then $A^c=X\setminus A\in\mathcal M$;
3. (σ3) if $A_i\in\mathcal M$ for $i=1,2,\dots$ then $\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i\in\mathcal M$.

The pair $(X,\mathcal M)$ is a *measurable space* and the members of $\mathcal M$ are the *measurable sets* (lecture 1, page 1).

Three consequences follow immediately. $\emptyset=X^c\in\mathcal M$. Countable intersections stay in $\mathcal M$ because $\bigcap_i A_i=\big(\bigcup_i A_i^c\big)^c$. Differences stay in $\mathcal M$ because $A\setminus B=A\cap B^c$. Compare this with a topology $\tau$ on $X$: $\tau$ is closed under *finite* intersections and *arbitrary* unions, but not under complements. A σ-algebra is closed under complements and under *countable* unions and intersections, and neither notion contains the other.

**Example.** $\mathcal P(X)$ is a σ-algebra, the largest; $\{\emptyset,X\}$ is a σ-algebra, the smallest. Every σ-algebra on $X$ sits between them.

**Example (countable–co-countable).** For any set $X$, $\mathcal M_c=\{A\subset X: A\text{ countable or }A^c\text{ countable}\}$ is a σ-algebra. Axioms (σ1) and (σ2) are immediate; (σ3) needs a case split, which is exercise 1. When $X$ is uncountable this σ-algebra is strictly smaller than $\mathcal P(X)$ and will be our first non-trivial example of measurability.

**Warning (an algebra that is not a σ-algebra).** When $X$ is infinite, the collection $\mathcal F$ of sets that are finite or have finite complement satisfies (σ1), (σ2) and is closed under *finite* unions, but it fails (σ3). Exercise 1 asks for the witness. A collection with (σ1), (σ2) and finite unions is called an *algebra*; the σ in σ-algebra stands for the countable operations, and they are the whole point.

**Proposition (generated σ-algebra).** For any collection $\mathcal F\subset\mathcal P(X)$ there is a smallest σ-algebra $\sigma(\mathcal F)$ containing $\mathcal F$ (lecture 1, Proposition 0.3).

**Proof.** Let $\Omega$ be the set of all σ-algebras on $X$ containing $\mathcal F$; it is nonempty because $\mathcal P(X)\in\Omega$. Put $\sigma(\mathcal F)=\bigcap_{\mathcal M\in\Omega}\mathcal M$. It contains $\mathcal F$, and it is a σ-algebra: if $A\in\sigma(\mathcal F)$ then $A$ lies in every $\mathcal M\in\Omega$, so $A^c$ does too, so $A^c\in\sigma(\mathcal F)$; countable unions are handled the same way, and $X$ lies in every member of $\Omega$. By construction $\sigma(\mathcal F)\subset\mathcal M$ for every σ-algebra $\mathcal M\supset\mathcal F$. $\square$

**Warning.** Unlike a topology generated by a basis, $\sigma(\mathcal F)$ has no explicit description in general. Proofs about it use the *good sets principle*: show that the sets with the desired property form a σ-algebra containing $\mathcal F$, hence containing $\sigma(\mathcal F)$. The next section uses it twice.

**Definition (Borel σ-algebra).** For a topological space $(X,\tau)$, $\mathcal B_X=\sigma(\tau)$ is the Borel σ-algebra and its members are the *Borel sets* (lecture 1, page 3). Open sets, closed sets, $F_\sigma$ sets (countable unions of closed sets) and $G_\delta$ sets (countable intersections of open sets) are all Borel. On $\mathbb R$ the Borel σ-algebra is generated by the open intervals with rational endpoints, since every open set is a countable union of them; it is also generated by the rays $(a,\infty)$, because $\bigcap_n(b-\tfrac1n,\infty)=[b,\infty)$ and so $(a,b)=(a,\infty)\cap\big(\bigcap_n(b-\tfrac1n,\infty)\big)^c$.

## Measurable functions

**Definition (measurable function).** Let $(X,\mathcal M)$ be a measurable space and $Y$ a topological space. A map $f:X\to Y$ is *measurable* if $f^{-1}(U)\in\mathcal M$ for every open $U\subset Y$ (lecture 1, page 1). The slogan parallels continuity: inverse images of open sets are measurable.

For us $Y$ is $\mathbb R$, $\mathbb C$, or the extended line $[-\infty,\infty]$ with the topology whose open sets are generated by $[-\infty,a)$, $(a,b)$ and $(b,\infty]$.

**Proposition.** If $f:X\to Y$ is measurable and $g:Y\to Z$ is continuous, then $g\circ f$ is measurable (lecture 1, Proposition 0.1(2)).

**Proof.** $(g\circ f)^{-1}(U)=f^{-1}(g^{-1}(U))$ and $g^{-1}(U)$ is open. $\square$

**Warning.** The order matters: *continuous after measurable* is measurable. Measurable after continuous, and measurable after measurable, can fail; the obstruction is a Lebesgue measurable set that is not Borel, which unit 5 constructs. What does survive is *Borel after measurable*, proved below.

**Theorem.** Let $u,v:X\to\mathbb R$ be measurable and $\Phi:\mathbb R^2\to Y$ continuous. Then $h(x)=\Phi(u(x),v(x))$ is measurable (lecture 1, Theorem 0.2).

**Proof.** Put $F=(u,v):X\to\mathbb R^2$. For an open rectangle $R=I_1\times I_2$ with open intervals $I_1,I_2$, $F^{-1}(R)=u^{-1}(I_1)\cap v^{-1}(I_2)\in\mathcal M$. Every open $U\subset\mathbb R^2$ is a countable union of rectangles with rational corners, so $F^{-1}(U)$ is a countable union of measurable sets and $F$ is measurable. Then $h=\Phi\circ F$ is measurable by the proposition. $\square$

The consequences are lecture 1's Examples (a)–(d). A complex function $f=u+iv$ is measurable if and only if $u$ and $v$ are (take $\Phi(s,t)=s+it$ one way, and $\operatorname{Re}$, $\operatorname{Im}$ the other); then $|f|$ is measurable. Sums and products of measurable real or complex functions are measurable ($\Phi(s,t)=s+t$ and $st$). A characteristic function $\chi_E$ is measurable if and only if $E\in\mathcal M$, since $\chi_E^{-1}(U)$ is one of $\emptyset,E,E^c,X$.

**Proposition (good sets for preimages).** For any $f:X\to Y$, the collection $\Omega=\{E\subset Y: f^{-1}(E)\in\mathcal M\}$ is a σ-algebra on $Y$. Consequently, if $f$ is measurable then $f^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal M$ for every Borel set $B\subset Y$ (lecture 2, Proposition 0.1(a), (b)).

**Proof.** $f^{-1}(Y)=X$, $f^{-1}(E^c)=f^{-1}(E)^c$ and $f^{-1}(\bigcup E_i)=\bigcup f^{-1}(E_i)$; so $\Omega$ inherits each axiom from $\mathcal M$. If $f$ is measurable, $\Omega$ contains the open sets, hence $\sigma(\tau_Y)=\mathcal B_Y$. $\square$

In particular, if $f$ is measurable and $\varphi:Y\to Z$ is Borel measurable (preimages of open sets are Borel), then $\varphi\circ f$ is measurable: that is the "Borel after measurable" promised above.

**Theorem (the $(\alpha,\infty]$ criterion).** A function $f:X\to[-\infty,\infty]$ is measurable if and only if $\{f>\alpha\}=f^{-1}((\alpha,\infty])\in\mathcal M$ for every $\alpha\in\mathbb R$ (lecture 2, Proposition 0.1(c)).

**Proof.** Necessity holds because $(\alpha,\infty]$ is open. For sufficiency, $\Omega$ contains every $(\alpha,\infty]$. Since $[-\infty,\alpha)=\bigcup_{n}\,(\alpha-\tfrac1n,\infty]^c$, $\Omega$ contains every $[-\infty,\alpha)$, and then every $(\alpha,\beta)=[-\infty,\beta)\cap(\alpha,\infty]$. Every open subset of $[-\infty,\infty]$ is a countable union of sets of these three kinds, so $\Omega$ contains all open sets and $f$ is measurable. $\square$

The same argument works with $\{f\ge\alpha\}$, $\{f<\alpha\}$ or $\{f\le\alpha\}$ in place of $\{f>\alpha\}$, and exercise 2 shows that rational $\alpha$ suffice. This criterion is how measurability is checked in practice.

**Theorem (countable limits).** If $f_n:X\to[-\infty,\infty]$ are measurable for $n=1,2,\dots$, then $\sup_n f_n$, $\inf_n f_n$, $\limsup_n f_n$ and $\liminf_n f_n$ are measurable. In particular a pointwise limit of measurable functions is measurable, and so are $\max\{f,g\}$ and $\min\{f,g\}$ (lecture 2, Theorem 0.3 and Corollary 0.4).

**Proof.** $\{\sup_n f_n>\alpha\}=\bigcup_n\{f_n>\alpha\}$: if the supremum exceeds $\alpha$ then some $f_n(x)$ does, and conversely. This is a countable union of measurable sets, so $\sup_n f_n$ is measurable by the criterion. Then $\inf_n f_n=-\sup_n(-f_n)$ and $\limsup_n f_n=\inf_k\sup_{n\ge k}f_n$. $\square$

**Warning.** Countability did the work: for an uncountable family $\{f_t\}_{t\in T}$ the set $\{\sup_t f_t>\alpha\}$ is an uncountable union and need not be measurable. This theorem is the first payoff of asking for countable unions in (σ3), and exercise 3 asks you to say so in your own words.

For real-valued $f$ write $f^+=\max\{f,0\}$ and $f^-=-\min\{f,0\}$. Both are measurable and nonnegative, $f=f^+-f^-$ and $|f|=f^++f^-$; the integral of a general function will be defined through this decomposition in unit 3.

## Simple functions

**Definition (simple function).** A function $s:X\to\mathbb R$ is *simple* if it takes only finitely many values, none of them $\pm\infty$. If $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n$ are its distinct values and $A_i=\{s=\alpha_i\}$ then $s=\sum_i\alpha_i\chi_{A_i}$, and $s$ is measurable if and only if every $A_i\in\mathcal M$ (lecture 2, page 3).

**Theorem (approximation from below).** Let $f:X\to[0,\infty]$ be measurable. There are measurable simple functions $0\le s_1\le s_2\le\cdots$ with $s_n(x)\to f(x)$ for every $x\in X$ (lecture 2, Theorem 0.5).

**Proof.** Define $\varphi_n:[0,\infty]\to[0,\infty)$ by $\varphi_n(t)=k2^{-n}$ when $k2^{-n}\le t<(k+1)2^{-n}$ and $t<n$, and $\varphi_n(t)=n$ when $t\ge n$. Each $\varphi_n$ is Borel measurable, since each of its finitely many level sets is an interval. Passing from $n$ to $n+1$ halves the mesh and raises the cut-off, which can only raise the value: $\varphi_n\le\varphi_{n+1}\le t$. If $t<\infty$ then $0\le t-\varphi_n(t)<2^{-n}$ as soon as $n>t$, and if $t=\infty$ then $\varphi_n(t)=n\to\infty$; so $\varphi_n(t)\to t$ for every $t$. Set $s_n=\varphi_n\circ f$. It is simple, it is measurable because it is Borel after measurable, it increases in $n$, and $s_n(x)=\varphi_n(f(x))\to f(x)$. $\square$

Notice the bonus: if $f$ is bounded by $M$, then for $n>M$ the error $f-s_n$ is below $2^{-n}$ everywhere, so the convergence is uniform. Integration in unit 2 will be defined through exactly these simple approximants.

## What to carry forward

A σ-algebra is the domain of a measure, closed under the countable operations and no more. Measurable functions are those whose preimages of open (equivalently Borel, equivalently $(\alpha,\infty]$) sets are measurable; they are stable under continuous post-composition, algebra, and countable suprema and limits. Nonnegative measurable functions are increasing limits of simple functions. Next unit: positive measures, the integral of a nonnegative function, and the monotone convergence theorem.
