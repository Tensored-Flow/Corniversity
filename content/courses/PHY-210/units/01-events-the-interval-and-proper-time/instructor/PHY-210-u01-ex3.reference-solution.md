## Reference explanation and acceptable alternatives (instructor only)

Explanation exercise; grade the ideas and give enough feedback to resolve the first substantive gap.

- Must contain: coordinate time between two events = difference of readings on two synchronised clocks of one frame, one at each event (needs a frame); proper time along the straight inertial route = reading of a uniformly moving clock present at both events, equal to $\sqrt{-\Delta s^2}/c$, the same number whoever computes it. The book's language (Section 1.3): "wristwatch time".
- The shorthand: "moving clocks run slow" compares the proper time of a clock with the coordinate time of a frame in which it moves. The clock is not defective and does not know it is moving; the asymmetry is between the two *quantities*, not between the two *observers*. A good answer notes the symmetry: each frame finds the other's clocks' proper times shorter than its own coordinate times. Do not require the twin-paradox discussion (unit 4).
- Example: any timelike pair with numbers, e.g. a muon created at $A$ and decaying at $B$ with $v = 0.98c$, laboratory $\Delta t = 11\ \mathrm{\mu s}$, proper time $11\sqrt{1-0.9604} = 2.2\ \mathrm{\mu s}$; the muon's frame measures proper time, the laboratory measures coordinate time. Any correct pair with frames named counts; the book's spark data is excluded by the prompt.
- Conceptual error to correct precisely: "proper time is the time in the observer's rest frame" (elapsed proper time belongs to a specified worldline; the endpoint formula here is for the inertial route).

## One possible learner explanation

A coordinate time interval compares readings on synchronised clocks of a specified inertial frame. Proper time is what a clock accumulates along its own worldline. For the uniformly moving path considered here, it equals the endpoint interval divided by c. For example, take events separated by 5 years and 3 light-years in the Earth frame. An inertial travelling clock reads 4 years between them; Earth-frame clocks at the two events differ by 5 years. Neither clock is defective. The comparison uses one clock on the travelling path and two synchronised clocks in the Earth frame. A different, accelerated path between the same events can have another proper time, so one must name the path as well as the endpoints.
