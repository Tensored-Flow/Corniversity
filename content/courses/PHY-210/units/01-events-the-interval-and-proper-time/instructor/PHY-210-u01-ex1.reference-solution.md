## Reference reasoning (instructor only)

Convention $(-,+,+,+)$: $\Delta s^2 = -c^2\Delta t^2 + \Delta x^2$.

**1.** Retain precision: $c\Delta t=10.00107639888\,\mathrm m$ and $\Delta s^2=36-(10.00107639888)^2=-64.0215291362\,\mathrm{m^2}$. This is timelike. Reporting $-64.0\,\mathrm{m^2}$ to three significant figures is reasonable, but do not use that rounded value to decide an exact null boundary.

**2.** The unrounded values are $v/c=0.599935423$, $c\tau=8.001345458\,\mathrm m$ and $\tau=26.68961558\,\mathrm{ns}$. An inertial clock present at both sparks must cover $6.00\ \mathrm m$ in $10.0\ \mathrm m$ of light-travel time: $v/c = 6.00/10.0 = 0.600$. In its frame $\Delta x' = 0$, so $-c^2\tau^2 = \Delta s^2$ and $c\tau = \sqrt{64.0}\ \mathrm m = 8.00\ \mathrm m$, i.e. $\tau = 8.00/0.299\,792\,458 = 26.7\ \mathrm{ns}$. This is the proper time (wristwatch time) between the sparks. Cross-check with the lesson's formula: $\Delta t\sqrt{1 - 0.36} = 10.0\times0.800 = 8.00\ \mathrm m$. $\checkmark$

**3.** No. In a frame where the sparks are simultaneous, $\Delta t' = 0$ and $\Delta s^2 = \Delta x'^2 \ge 0$; but $\Delta s^2 = -64\ \mathrm{m^2} < 0$ in every frame. Timelike pairs have a frame-independent time order.

**4(a).** The revised prompt specifies $\Delta x=c\Delta t$ exactly, so the separation is null. Neither co-location nor simultaneity is possible in a finite inertial frame for these distinct events; either would require a boost at $c$. The path of a vacuum light signal has zero proper time, but no photon rest clock exists. If one instead used exactly $10.0\,\mathrm m$ with the supplied time, the interval would be about $-0.0215291\,\mathrm{m^2}$, timelike rather than exactly null. With finite measurement uncertainty, data compatible with zero do not establish exact nullness.

**4(b).** Before rounding, the squared interval is $43.9784708638\,\mathrm{m^2}$, the simultaneity boost is $v/c=0.83342303324$, and proper distance is $6.631626562\,\mathrm m$. At the reporting precision below these become $44.0$, $0.833$ and $6.63$. $\Delta x = 12.0\ \mathrm m$: $\Delta s^2 = -100 + 144 = +44\ \mathrm{m^2}$, spacelike. No co-moving frame ($v$ would be $1.2c$). A frame moving at $v = c^2\Delta t/\Delta x$, i.e. $v/c = 10.0/12.0 = 0.833$, sees them simultaneous; in it $\Delta x' = \sqrt{44}\ \mathrm m = 6.63\ \mathrm m$, the proper distance. (With the Lorentz transformation of unit 2: $\Delta t' = \gamma(\Delta t - v\Delta x/c^2) = 0$ gives the same $v$; $\Delta x' = \gamma(\Delta x - v\Delta t) = (12 - 10\cdot 10/12)/\sqrt{1 - 100/144} = (12 - 8.333)/0.5528 = 6.63\ \mathrm m$. $\checkmark$)

**Alternative valid approaches.** Working entirely in the book's convention is fine if stated. Using $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2} = 1.25$ and $\tau = \Delta t/\gamma$ for part 2 is fine. For 4(b), a learner who has not met the Lorentz transformation may cite the lesson's formula $v = c^2\Delta t/\Delta x$ and then get $\Delta x'$ from invariance; both routes are acceptable.

**Common gaps.** Forgetting to convert nanoseconds to metres (then adding $\mathrm{ns^2}$ to $\mathrm m^2$); reporting $\tau$ in metres only when the question asks for nanoseconds too; concluding that a lightlike pair has a co-moving frame "moving at $c$"; in 4(b), giving $v = 1.2c$ as if it were a frame.
