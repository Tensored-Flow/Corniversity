## Reference reasoning (instructor only)

**1. Log-likelihood.** $\ln P(y_t \mid \lambda_t) = y_t\ln\lambda_t - \lambda_t - \ln y_t!$ with $\ln\lambda_t = \mathbf{x}_t^\top\boldsymbol\theta$. Summing and dropping $\sum\ln y_t!$:
$$\ell(\boldsymbol\theta) = \sum_t\big[y_t\,\mathbf{x}_t^\top\boldsymbol\theta - e^{\mathbf{x}_t^\top\boldsymbol\theta}\big] = \mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\theta - \mathbf{1}^\top e^{\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\theta} = \mathbf{y}^\top\ln\boldsymbol\lambda - \mathbf{1}^\top\boldsymbol\lambda,$$
the tutorial's form.

**2. Gradient, Hessian, concavity.** $\nabla\ell = \sum_t(y_t - \lambda_t)\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y} - \boldsymbol\lambda)$. $\nabla^2\ell = -\sum_t\lambda_t\mathbf{x}_t\mathbf{x}_t^\top = -\mathbf{X}^\top\text{diag}(\boldsymbol\lambda)\mathbf{X}$. For any $\mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{v}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\text{diag}(\boldsymbol\lambda)\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v} = \sum_t\lambda_t(\mathbf{x}_t^\top\mathbf{v})^2 \ge 0$ since $\lambda_t > 0$; so the Hessian is negative semidefinite everywhere and $\ell$ is concave. If $\mathbf{X}$ has full column rank, $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v} \ne 0$ for $\mathbf{v} \ne 0$, the form is strictly positive, $\ell$ is strictly concave and the maximiser (if it exists) is unique. Existence can fail with separable data (weights diverging), which regularisation cures.

**3. Moment matching.** $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}^\top\boldsymbol\lambda$: for each input column, the input-weighted sum of observed counts equals the input-weighted sum of predicted rates. Intercept only: $\sum_t(y_t - e^{\theta_0}) = 0 \Rightarrow e^{\theta_0} = \bar y$, $\theta_0 = \ln\bar y$. The best constant-rate model predicts the mean count, exactly as in unit 2's laboratory baseline, now derived from the GLM.

**4. Interpretation.** $\lambda = \exp(\theta_0 + \sum_k\theta_kx_k)$, so increasing $x_k$ by one unit multiplies $\lambda$ by $e^{\theta_k}$ irrespective of the other inputs: effects compound multiplicatively because the nonlinearity is exponential. The Linear-Gaussian model, $\lambda = \mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\theta$, adds $\theta_k$ to the rate; its two defects are that it can predict negative rates and that it models integer counts as continuous Gaussian variables with constant variance, whereas Poisson counts have variance equal to the mean.

**5. Assumptions a real spike train violates.** Conditional independence of bins given the stimulus: refractoriness and bursting make successive bins dependent; residuals $y_t - \lambda_t$ show a negative dip right after spikes (or positive autocorrelation for bursts); remedy: add the cell's own spike history as design-matrix columns (the GLM with spike-history filter). Stationarity: adaptation or slow drift produces residuals that trend over the recording; remedy: slow covariates or a time-varying intercept. Linearity in the exponent: strong stimuli saturate; remedy: a different nonlinearity or basis expansion. Poisson variance: overdispersion; remedy: negative-binomial likelihood.

**Alternative valid approaches.** Writing the Hessian as $-\sum_t\lambda_t\mathbf{x}_t\mathbf{x}_t^\top$ and arguing semidefiniteness from a sum of rank-one PSD matrices; using the exponential-family general result that the canonical-link log-likelihood is concave. Accept either.

**Common gaps.** Sign errors giving $\mathbf{X}^\top(\boldsymbol\lambda - \mathbf{y})$ as the gradient of $\ell$ (it is the gradient of $-\ell$); calling the log-likelihood convex; the intercept MLE given as $\bar y$ for $\theta_0$ rather than for $\lambda$; forgetting that uniqueness needs full column rank.
