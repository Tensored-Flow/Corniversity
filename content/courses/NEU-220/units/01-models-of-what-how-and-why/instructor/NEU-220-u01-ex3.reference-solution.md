## Reference reasoning (instructor only)

**1. The maximum-entropy theorem.** Let $p$ be a density on $[0, \infty)$ with $\int\tau p = \mu$, and $q(\tau) = \mu^{-1}e^{-\tau/\mu}$. Wherever $p > 0$ we have $q > 0$, so the Kullback–Leibler divergence $D(p\,\|\,q) = \int p\ln(p/q) \ge 0$ is well defined, with equality iff $p = q$ almost everywhere (Gibbs' inequality, itself from $\ln x \le x - 1$). Then
$$h(p) = -\int p\ln p = -\int p\ln q - D(p\,\|\,q) \le -\int p\ln q.$$
Since $\ln q(\tau) = -\ln\mu - \tau/\mu$ is affine in $\tau$, $-\int p\ln q = \ln\mu + \mu^{-1}\int\tau p = \ln\mu + 1$, the same for every $p$ of mean $\mu$. So $h(p) \le 1 + \ln\mu = h(q)$, with equality iff $p = q$ a.e.

Lagrangian route: maximise $-\int p\ln p$ subject to $\int p = 1$ and $\int\tau p = \mu$; stationarity gives $-\ln p - 1 - \alpha - \beta\tau = 0$, so $p \propto e^{-\beta\tau}$, and the constraints fix $p = q$. Because $-\int p\ln p$ is strictly concave in $p$ and the constraints are affine, the stationary point is the unique maximum; a learner who omits this justification has found a candidate, not a maximum.

**2. Comparisons.** Uniform on $[0, 2\mu]$: $h = \ln(2\mu) = \ln 2 + \ln\mu$. Shortfall: $(1 + \ln\mu) - (\ln 2 + \ln\mu) = 1 - \ln 2 = 0.307$ nats, independent of $\mu$. A density concentrating at $\tau = \mu$ (e.g. uniform on $[\mu - \epsilon, \mu + \epsilon]$, $h = \ln 2\epsilon \to -\infty$) has entropy decreasing without bound; the deterministic neuron carries no information in its intervals. Tutorial 3's three toy distributions illustrate exactly this ordering.

**3. Interpretation (model answer, 160 words).** The theorem supports a normative claim: if a neuron's intervals were arranged to maximise entropy, hence the information capacity of the interval code, under the single constraint of a fixed mean rate and with every nonnegative interval available, its ISI distribution would be exponential. The constraint set is the whole content of the claim. Recorded histograms, such as the Steinmetz ISIs in Tutorial 1, often show a gap below a few milliseconds and sometimes bimodality or a CV far from 1; each counts against the claim as stated. The response that keeps the objective is to add the constraints the neuron actually faces: with a refractory period the admissible support is $[t_{ref}, \infty)$ and the maximiser becomes the shifted exponential of exercise 1, which predicts the gap. Bimodality would need a further constraint or a different objective. A normative model is refined by making its constraints honest, and abandoned when no plausible constraint set rescues the objective.

**Alternative valid approaches.** Jensen's inequality applied to $\int p\ln(q/p) \le \ln\int q = 0$; a calculus-of-variations argument with the second variation checked. Accept entropy in bits (then the shortfall is $(1 - \ln 2)/\ln 2 = 0.443$ bits); the numeric check expects nats, so note the unit if the learner used bits.

**Common gaps.** Proving only the finite-support or discrete case; asserting the Lagrange stationary point is a maximum without concavity; computing $h(\text{uniform}) = \ln\mu$ (forgetting the interval length is $2\mu$); reading the theorem as "neurons are exponential".
