## Lesson notes (instructor only)

Source mapping, MIT 8.09 Chapter 1 notes (Fall 2014, Iain Stewart), PDF page = physical index + 1, no PDF labels: Newtonian mechanics and Galilean transformations pp. 1–2; polar coordinates Eqs. 1.1–1.2 and Lagrangian Eqs. 1.3–1.4 p. 3; Hamilton's principle Eq. 1.5 and the point-transformation proof Eqs. 1.6–1.13 pp. 4–5; examples with generalized coordinates Eqs. 1.14–1.18 pp. 5–6; cone Eqs. 1.19–1.23 p. 7; Hamiltonian mechanics Eqs. 1.24–1.28 p. 8; calculus of variations Eqs. 1.76–1.78 p. 22. Tong, Classical Dynamics, Section 2.1 (reference-only) gives the same derivation with a longer discussion of least action.

Teaching intent: install two habits — carry the units to the last line, and take a limiting case in which the answer is already known. The learner has an Imperial mathematics background, so the calculus of variations will be familiar; spend the tutorial time on the physics (which coordinate to eliminate, what the conserved quantity means, why the flat limit kills circular orbits) rather than on the integration by parts. If the learner finishes exercise 1 quickly, offer the optional radial-oscillation extension in the reference solution and ask them to predict the factor of 2 before computing it.

Watch for: using the Euler–Lagrange equations on $(r,\theta,z)$ with the constraint still in force (the independence error); writing $m\ddot r = F_r$ in polar coordinates; describing Hamilton's principle as a minimum principle.

Staged misconception for a peer discussion, if peers are enabled: a classmate may claim that $L_3$ in exercise 2 is "just a gauge choice". The tutor should resolve it through the equation of motion $m\ddot x = 2cx$, not by authority.
