## Reference reasoning (instructor only)

Throughout, $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ and, for $A\in GL_2(\mathbb R)$, $A^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det A}\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}$.

**(a) Upper triangular invertible matrices: a subgroup.** $I$ is upper triangular. The product of $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\0&d\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix}a'&b'\\0&d'\end{pmatrix}$ is $\begin{pmatrix}aa'&ab'+bd'\\0&dd'\end{pmatrix}$, upper triangular (and invertible as a product of invertibles). The inverse of $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\0&d\end{pmatrix}$ ($ad\neq0$) is $\begin{pmatrix}1/a&-b/(ad)\\0&1/d\end{pmatrix}$, upper triangular.

**(b) $\det=\pm1$: a subgroup.** $\det I=1$; $\det(AB)=\det A\det B\in\{\pm1\}$ when both factors have determinant $\pm1$; $\det(A^{-1})=1/\det A=\pm1$.

**(c) Integer entries: not a subgroup.** $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ has integer entries and $\det A=2\neq0$, but $A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ does not. The inverse condition fails (closure and identity hold).

**(d) Integer entries with $\det=\pm1$: a subgroup ($GL_2(\mathbb Z)$).** $I$ qualifies. Products of integer matrices are integer matrices and determinants multiply, so closure holds. For inverses, $A^{-1}=\frac1{\det A}\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}=\pm\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}$, which has integer entries and determinant $\pm1$.

**(e) The centraliser: a subgroup.** $IB=BI$. If $AB=BA$ and $A'B=BA'$ then $(AA')B=A(BA')=(AB)A'=B(AA')$. If $AB=BA$, multiply on the left and right by $A^{-1}$: $A^{-1}(AB)A^{-1}=A^{-1}(BA)A^{-1}$ gives $BA^{-1}=A^{-1}B$. So $C(B)\le G$.

**(f) $GL_2(\mathbb Z_2)$.** A $2\times2$ matrix over a field is invertible iff its columns are linearly independent. The first column may be any nonzero vector of $\mathbb Z_2^2$: $4-1=3$ choices. The second column may be any vector outside the span of the first, which is $\{0,v\}$: $4-2=2$ choices. Hence $|GL_2(\mathbb Z_2)|=3\cdot2=6$. (Listing: $I$, $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$.) Non-abelian: with $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $Q=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, working mod $2$,
$$PQ=\begin{pmatrix}1+1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad QP=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1+1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},$$
so $PQ\neq QP$. (In unit 5 the learner can show $GL_2(\mathbb Z_2)\cong S_3$: it permutes the three nonzero vectors of $\mathbb Z_2^2$.)

**Alternative valid approaches.** Any of (a), (b), (d), (e) may be done with the one-step test $gh^{-1}\in H$. For (f), counting via $|GL_n(\mathbb F_q)|=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(q^n-q^i)$ is fine if the learner proves the formula for $n=2$; an exhaustive list of the $16$ matrices with determinants computed is acceptable for the count but should be marked partially met on the structural reason unless the independence argument is also given.

**Common gaps.** In (c) giving a non-invertible integer matrix as the witness (it is not in $G$ to begin with); in (d) asserting inverses are integral "because $\det=\pm1$" without the adjugate; in (e) proving closure but not inverses; in (f) counting $15$ (all nonzero matrices) or $16$, or forgetting to reduce mod $2$ in the products.
