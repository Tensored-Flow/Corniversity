## Reference reasoning (instructor only)

**1. Exponent two forces commutativity.** If $g^2=e$ for all $g$, then every element is its own inverse: $g^{-1}=g$. For $a,b\in G$, apply this to $ab$ and use Proposition 3.2.12:
$$ab=(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}=ba.$$
So $G$ is abelian. (The hypothesis is used twice: for $ab$ and for $a$, $b$ separately.)

**2. Even order gives a nontrivial involution.** Partition $G$ into the sets $\{g,g^{-1}\}$. Such a set has two elements when $g\neq g^{-1}$, i.e. when $g^2\neq e$, and one element when $g^2=e$. Let $k$ be the number of elements with $g^2=e$ and $m$ the number of two-element pairs; then $|G|=k+2m$. If $|G|$ is even then $k$ is even. Since $e^2=e$, $k\ge1$, hence $k\ge2$: there is $g\neq e$ with $g^2=e$. $\square$

This is the case $p=2$ of Cauchy's theorem (unit 10); the pairing proof needs nothing beyond the axioms, which is why it is set now.

**3. The set of involutions.** Let $H=\{g:g^2=e\}$.

*Abelian case.* $e\in H$. If $a,b\in H$ then, using commutativity, $(ab)^2=abab=aabb=a^2b^2=e$, so $ab\in H$. If $a\in H$ then $a^{-1}=a\in H$. By Proposition 3.3.7, $H\le G$.

*Counterexample.* In $S_3$, the symmetries of an equilateral triangle, $H$ consists of $e$ and the three reflections (transpositions), so $|H|=4$. Two distinct reflections compose to a nontrivial rotation: in cycle notation (composing right to left) $(1\,2)(1\,3)$ sends $1\mapsto3\mapsto3$, $3\mapsto1\mapsto2$, $2\mapsto2\mapsto1$, so $(1\,2)(1\,3)=(1\,3\,2)$, which has order $3$ and is not in $H$. Thus $H$ is not closed and is not a subgroup. (Any convention for composing permutations gives a $3$-cycle; the learner's convention should be stated.)

Where commutativity entered: the step $abab=aabb$. In $S_3$ it is exactly this step that fails for two reflections.

**Alternative valid approaches.** Part 1 can be done by expanding $(ab)^2=e$ as $abab=e$ and multiplying on the left by $a$ and on the right by $b$: $ba=a(abab)b=ab$. Part 2 is sometimes argued by counting elements of order $>2$ in pairs $\{g,g^{-1}\}$; same proof. Valid counterexamples for part 3: any $D_n$ with $n\ge3$ (two reflections compose to a rotation), any $S_n$ with $n\ge3$, $GL_2(\mathbb R)$ (two reflections of the plane), $GL_2(\mathbb Z_2)$ from exercise 2. Invalid "counterexamples" to watch for: $Q_8$ (there $H=\{\pm1\}$, a subgroup) and $A_4$ (there $H=\{e\}\cup\{\text{double transpositions}\}$ is the Klein four-group, a subgroup); point out the computation that shows $H$ is in fact closed in those groups.

**Common gaps.** In part 1 writing $(ab)^{-1}=a^{-1}b^{-1}$; in part 2 forgetting that $e$ is one of the self-paired elements (so $k$ even gives $k\ge2$, not merely $k\ge1$), or invoking Lagrange's theorem, which arrives in unit 4; in part 3 naming a group without computing the offending product.
