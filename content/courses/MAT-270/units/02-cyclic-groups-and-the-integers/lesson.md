## The integers, revisited

Everything in this unit runs on one piece of arithmetic, so we start there.

**Theorem (division algorithm).** For integers $a$ and $b>0$ there are unique integers $q,r$ with $a=bq+r$ and $0\le r<b$ (Theorem 2.2.1).

**Theorem (Bézout).** For nonzero integers $a,b$ there are integers $r,s$ with $\gcd(a,b)=ar+bs$ (Theorem 2.2.2). In particular, if $\gcd(a,b)=1$ there are $r,s$ with $ar+bs=1$ (Corollary 2.2.3).

**Proof.** The set $S=\{ax+by: x,y\in\mathbb Z,\ ax+by>0\}$ is nonempty (it contains $|a|$), so it has a least element $d=ar+bs$. Divide: $a=dq+r'$ with $0\le r'<d$; then $r'=a-dq=a(1-rq)+b(-sq)$ is of the form $ax+by$, and $r'<d$ forces $r'=0$ by minimality. So $d\mid a$, and likewise $d\mid b$. Any common divisor of $a$ and $b$ divides $ar+bs=d$. Hence $d$ is the greatest common divisor. $\square$

**Lemma (Euclid).** If a prime $p$ divides $ab$ then $p\mid a$ or $p\mid b$ (Lemma 2.2.5). **Proof.** If $p\nmid a$ then $\gcd(p,a)=1$, so $1=pr+as$ for some $r,s$, and $b=pbr+(ab)s$ is divisible by $p$. $\square$

In unit 1 Bézout gave the units criterion for $\mathbb Z_n$; here it gives the structure of cyclic groups.

## Cyclic subgroups and orders

**Definition (cyclic subgroup).** For $a\in G$, $\langle a\rangle=\{a^k:k\in\mathbb Z\}$. **Theorem.** $\langle a\rangle$ is a subgroup of $G$, and it is the smallest subgroup containing $a$ (Theorem 4.1.3).

**Proof.** $e=a^0\in\langle a\rangle$; $a^ka^l=a^{k+l}$ and $(a^k)^{-1}=a^{-k}$ by the exponent laws, so the subgroup test applies. Any subgroup containing $a$ contains all powers of $a$ by closure under products and inverses. $\square$

$G$ is *cyclic* if $G=\langle a\rangle$ for some $a$, called a *generator*. In additive notation $\langle a\rangle=\{na:n\in\mathbb Z\}$ (Remark 4.1.4).

**Proposition (order of an element).** Let $a\in G$. If $a^k\neq e$ for all $k>0$ then the powers $a^k$, $k\in\mathbb Z$, are pairwise distinct and $\langle a\rangle$ is infinite. Otherwise let $n$ be the least positive integer with $a^n=e$, the *order* of $a$; then $\langle a\rangle=\{e,a,\dots,a^{n-1}\}$ with these $n$ elements distinct, so $|\langle a\rangle|=n$, and $a^k=e$ if and only if $n\mid k$ (Proposition 4.1.12).

**Proof.** If $a^i=a^j$ with $i<j$ then $a^{j-i}=e$ with $j-i>0$; this proves the first claim. In the second case, for any $k$ write $k=nq+r$ with $0\le r<n$; then $a^k=(a^n)^qa^r=a^r$, so every power is one of $e,a,\dots,a^{n-1}$, and these are distinct because $a^i=a^j$ with $0\le i<j<n$ would give $a^{j-i}=e$ with $0<j-i<n$, against minimality. Finally $a^k=e$ iff $a^r=e$ iff $r=0$ iff $n\mid k$. $\square$

**Example.** In $\mathbb Z_6$, $\langle1\rangle=\langle5\rangle=\mathbb Z_6$ while $\langle2\rangle=\{0,2,4\}$, so $2$ has order $3$ (Example 4.1.5). $U(9)=\{1,2,4,5,7,8\}$ is cyclic: the powers of $2$ are $2,4,8,7,5,1$ (Example 4.1.6). $S_3$ is not cyclic, since no element has order $6$, although every proper subgroup of $S_3$ is cyclic (Example 4.1.7).

**Theorem.** Every cyclic group is abelian (Theorem 4.1.9): $a^ma^n=a^{m+n}=a^na^m$. The converse fails: $\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_2$ and $U(8)$ are abelian and not cyclic.

**Theorem.** Every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic (Theorem 4.1.10).

**Proof.** Let $G=\langle a\rangle$ and $H\le G$. If $H=\{e\}$ it is cyclic. Otherwise $H$ contains some $a^k$ with $k\neq0$, hence (taking inverses) with $k>0$; let $m$ be the least positive integer with $a^m\in H$. Claim $H=\langle a^m\rangle$. Clearly $\langle a^m\rangle\subset H$. Conversely let $a^k\in H$ and write $k=mq+r$ with $0\le r<m$; then $a^r=a^k(a^m)^{-q}\in H$, and minimality of $m$ forces $r=0$, so $a^k=(a^m)^q\in\langle a^m\rangle$. $\square$

The division algorithm did all the work. **Corollary.** The subgroups of $\mathbb Z$ are exactly the sets $n\mathbb Z$, $n\ge0$ (Corollary 4.1.11). Notice also that if $G$ is finite of order $n$, then $a^n=e\in H$, so by the same division argument $m\mid n$: the generator of a subgroup can be taken to be $a^m$ with $m$ a divisor of $n$. Exercise 1 pushes this to a complete description of the subgroups of a finite cyclic group.

**Theorem (order formula).** Let $G=\langle a\rangle$ have order $n$ and let $b=a^k$. Then $b$ has order $n/\gcd(k,n)$ (Theorem 4.1.13).

**Proof.** Put $d=\gcd(k,n)$, $k=dk'$, $n=dn'$, so $\gcd(k',n')=1$. By Proposition 4.1.12, $b^m=a^{km}=e$ iff $n\mid km$ iff $dn'\mid dk'm$ iff $n'\mid k'm$ iff $n'\mid m$, the last step because $\gcd(k',n')=1$ (Euclid's lemma applied to the prime factors of $n'$, or Bézout directly). The least positive such $m$ is $n'=n/d$. $\square$

**Corollary.** The generators of $\mathbb Z_n$ are the $r\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}$ with $\gcd(r,n)=1$, so $\mathbb Z_n$ has $\varphi(n)$ generators, where $\varphi$ is Euler's function (Corollary 4.1.14). For example $\mathbb Z_{16}$ is generated by $1,3,5,7,9,11,13,15$ (Example 4.1.15).

**Warning.** The order of $a^k$ is $n/\gcd(k,n)$, not $n/k$: in $\mathbb Z_6$ the element $4=4\cdot1$ has order $6/\gcd(4,6)=3$.

## Complex numbers and roots of unity

A nonzero complex number has polar form $z=r\operatorname{cis}\theta=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$ with $r=|z|>0$. **Proposition.** $(r\operatorname{cis}\theta)(s\operatorname{cis}\phi)=rs\operatorname{cis}(\theta+\phi)$ (Proposition 4.2.5), by the addition formulae. **Theorem (De Moivre).** $(r\operatorname{cis}\theta)^n=r^n\operatorname{cis}(n\theta)$ for $n\ge1$ (Theorem 4.2.7), by induction from the proposition.

**Proposition.** The *circle group* $\mathbb T=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z|=1\}$ is a subgroup of $\mathbb C^*$ (Proposition 4.2.9): $|zw|=|z||w|$ and $|z^{-1}|=|z|^{-1}$.

**Theorem (roots of unity).** For $n\ge1$ the equation $z^n=1$ has exactly $n$ solutions in $\mathbb C$, namely $\operatorname{cis}(2\pi k/n)$ for $k=0,1,\dots,n-1$, and they form a cyclic subgroup $\mu_n$ of $\mathbb T$ of order $n$, generated by $\omega=\operatorname{cis}(2\pi/n)$ (Theorem 4.2.10).

**Proof.** By De Moivre, $\operatorname{cis}(2\pi k/n)^n=\operatorname{cis}(2\pi k)=1$, and these $n$ numbers are distinct because their arguments $2\pi k/n$ are distinct points of $[0,2\pi)$. Conversely if $z^n=1$ then $|z|^n=1$, so $|z|=1$ and $z=\operatorname{cis}\theta$ with $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$; De Moivre gives $\operatorname{cis}(n\theta)=1$, so $n\theta=2\pi k$ for an integer $k$, and $0\le\theta<2\pi$ forces $0\le k<n$. So the solutions are exactly the $n$ listed numbers, which are $\omega^k$ for $k=0,\dots,n-1$ by De Moivre again. Hence the solution set is $\langle\omega\rangle$, a cyclic subgroup of $\mathbb T$, and $\omega$ has order exactly $n$. $\square$

(The text's proof cites a later corollary that a polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n$ roots; the argument above avoids it.)

A *primitive* $n$th root of unity is a generator of $\mu_n$. By the order formula, $\omega^k$ is primitive if and only if $\gcd(k,n)=1$, so there are $\varphi(n)$ of them; the primitive $8$th roots are $\omega,\omega^3,\omega^5,\omega^7$ with $\omega=\frac{\sqrt2}2(1+i)$ (Example 4.2.11). Exercise 2 studies how the groups $\mu_m$ and $\mu_n$ sit inside each other.

**Remark (repeated squares).** To compute $a^k$ in $U(n)$ one writes $k$ in binary and squares repeatedly, needing about $2\log_2k$ multiplications instead of $k$ (Section 4.3, Example 4.3.1). It is how orders in $U(n)$ are found in practice and is optional reading here.

**Warning.** $U(n)$ need not be cyclic. In $U(8)=\{1,3,5,7\}$ one has $3^2=9\equiv1$, $5^2=25\equiv1$, $7^2=49\equiv1$: every element has order at most $2$, so nothing generates a group of order $4$. By exercise 1 of unit 1, $U(8)$ is abelian for a second reason as well. Exercise 3 asks you to say exactly which property of cyclic groups fails here.

**Warning.** "Cyclic" does not mean "finite". $\mathbb Z=\langle1\rangle$ is cyclic, and it has exactly two generators, $1$ and $-1$: if $d$ generates $\mathbb Z$ then $1=dk$ for some integer $k$.

## What to carry forward

Bézout's identity and the division algorithm control the subgroups of cyclic groups: every subgroup is cyclic, generated by a power whose exponent divides the order, and the order of $a^k$ is $n/\gcd(k,n)$. The $n$th roots of unity are the concrete cyclic group of order $n$, and $U(n)$ is sometimes cyclic and sometimes not. Next unit: permutations, cycle notation and parity, and the dihedral groups.
