## Reference reasoning (instructor only)

Let $G=\langle a\rangle$, $|G|=n$, so $a$ has order $n$ and $a^k=e\iff n\mid k$ (Proposition 4.1.12).

**1. One subgroup per divisor.** Let $H\le G$, $H\neq\{e\}$. As in the proof of Theorem 4.1.10, $H=\langle a^m\rangle$ where $m$ is the least positive integer with $a^m\in H$. Since $a^n=e\in H$, write $n=mq+r$ with $0\le r<m$; then $a^r=a^n(a^m)^{-q}\in H$, so $r=0$ by minimality: $m\mid n$. By Theorem 4.1.13, $|H|=|a^m|=n/\gcd(m,n)=n/m$. Hence every subgroup has order dividing $n$ (this is Lagrange's theorem for cyclic groups, proved directly), and a subgroup of order $d$ must have $m=n/d$, i.e. $H=\langle a^{n/d}\rangle$: at most one subgroup of each order. Existence: $\langle a^{n/d}\rangle$ has order $n/\gcd(n/d,n)=n/(n/d)=d$. The trivial subgroup is the case $d=1$. $\square$

**2. Elements of order $d$.** Let $d\mid n$ and let $C_d=\langle a^{n/d}\rangle$ be the unique subgroup of order $d$. If $b\in G$ has order $d$ then $\langle b\rangle$ is a subgroup of order $d$, so $\langle b\rangle=C_d$ and $b$ is a generator of $C_d$. Conversely every generator of $C_d$ has order $|C_d|=d$. So the elements of order $d$ are exactly the generators of the cyclic group $C_d$ of order $d$, and by Corollary 4.1.14 (equivalently Theorem 4.1.13: $(a^{n/d})^j$ has order $d/\gcd(j,d)$, which equals $d$ iff $\gcd(j,d)=1$, for $j\in\{1,\dots,d\}$) there are $\varphi(d)$ of them. $\square$

**3. The identity.** Every element of $G$ has a unique order, and by part 1 that order divides $n$. Sorting the $n$ elements by order and using part 2, $n=\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)$. $\square$

**4. $\mathbb Z_{36}$.** Divisors of $36$: $1,2,3,4,6,9,12,18,36$. Values of $\varphi$: $\varphi(1)=1$, $\varphi(2)=1$, $\varphi(3)=2$, $\varphi(4)=2$, $\varphi(6)=2$, $\varphi(9)=6$, $\varphi(12)=4$, $\varphi(18)=6$, $\varphi(36)=12$. Sum: $1+1+2+2+2+6+4+6+12=36$. For instance the $12$ generators are the $r<36$ coprime to $36$: $1,5,7,11,13,17,19,23,25,29,31,35$; the elements of order $9$ are $4,8,16,20,28,32$ (the multiples of $4$ not divisible by $12$).

**Alternative valid approaches.** Part 1 may be organised around the lattice: $\langle a^k\rangle=\langle a^{\gcd(k,n)}\rangle$, so subgroups correspond to divisors. Part 2 may be done directly: $a^k$ has order $d$ iff $\gcd(k,n)=n/d$ iff $k=(n/d)j$ with $\gcd(j,d)=1$, $1\le j\le d$, giving $\varphi(d)$ values of $k$ in $\{1,\dots,n\}$. Part 3 is sometimes proved via the multiplicativity of $\varphi$; that is a different (harder) route and the exercise asks for the deduction from part 2, so accept it only if correct and complete, with a note.

**Common gaps.** Omitting the divisibility $m\mid n$ (so uniqueness is unsupported); asserting "$C_d$ has $\varphi(d)$ generators" without noting $C_d$ is cyclic of order $d$; in the table, $\varphi(9)=6$ and $\varphi(18)=6$ are the usual slips ($\varphi(9)=9-3$, $\varphi(18)=\varphi(2)\varphi(9)$).
