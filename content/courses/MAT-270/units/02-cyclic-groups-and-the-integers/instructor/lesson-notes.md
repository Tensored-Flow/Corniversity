## Lesson notes (instructor only)

Source mapping, Judson, Abstract Algebra: Theory and Applications, HTML edition (judsonbooks.org). Section 2.2: Theorem 2.2.1 (division algorithm), Theorem 2.2.2 (gcd and Bézout), Corollary 2.2.3, Example 2.2.4 (Euclidean algorithm), Lemma 2.2.5 (Euclid), Theorems 2.2.6–2.2.7. Section 4.1: Theorem 4.1.3, Remark 4.1.4, Examples 4.1.5–4.1.7, Theorem 4.1.9, Theorem 4.1.10, Corollary 4.1.11, Proposition 4.1.12, Theorem 4.1.13, Corollary 4.1.14, Example 4.1.15. Section 4.2: Proposition 4.2.5, Theorem 4.2.7 (De Moivre), Proposition 4.2.9, Theorem 4.2.10, Example 4.2.11. Section 4.3: Example 4.3.1.

Two deliberate departures from the text. (1) The text defines the order of $a$ as $|\langle a\rangle|$ and proves $a^k=e\iff n\mid k$ in Proposition 4.1.12; the lesson defines the order as the least positive exponent and proves the two agree, because the learner's exercises use both descriptions. (2) The text's proof of Theorem 4.2.10 cites Corollary 17.2.4 (a polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n$ roots) from the field-theory chapters; the lesson gives a direct argument via $|z|=1$ and the argument of $z$. Mention this if the learner compares proofs.

Teaching intent: Bézout is the single tool; make the learner say "Bézout" out loud at each use (units criterion, Euclid's lemma, exercise 2 part 1). Exercise 1 is Lagrange's theorem in miniature for cyclic groups; when unit 4 arrives, point back to it. Exercise 1 part 4 and exercise 3 catch arithmetic slips quickly; do not let a slip in $\varphi(9)$ hide a correct argument.

If the learner asks which $U(n)$ are cyclic: exactly $n\in\{1,2,4,p^k,2p^k\}$ for odd primes $p$. This is a cited theorem of number theory, not proved in this course; label it so.

Supplement: MIT RES.18-011 lecture 2 (student notes, unchecked by MIT faculty) covers subgroups of $\mathbb Z$ and cyclic groups; optional, never the source of a cited statement here.
