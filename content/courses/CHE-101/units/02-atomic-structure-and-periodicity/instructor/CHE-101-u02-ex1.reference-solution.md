## Reference reasoning (instructor only)

**1. The $3 \to 2$ transition.** $\Delta E = R_H\left(\tfrac{1}{2^2} - \tfrac{1}{3^2}\right) = 2.179\times10^{-18}\times\tfrac{5}{36} = 3.026\times10^{-19}$ J. $\lambda = hc/\Delta E = (6.626\times10^{-34})(2.998\times10^{8})/3.026\times10^{-19} = 6.564\times10^{-7}$ m $= 656.4$ nm. Visible, red (the Balmer $\alpha$ line; the lesson's example was $4 \to 2$ at 486 nm).

**2. Ionisation energy.** From $n = 1$ to $n = \infty$: $\Delta E = R_H = 2.179\times10^{-18}$ J per atom. Per mole: $2.179\times10^{-18}\times6.022\times10^{23} = 1.312\times10^{6}$ J mol$^{-1}$ $= 1312$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ (13.6 eV per atom).

**3. A 400 nm photon.** $E = hc/\lambda = 1.986\times10^{-25}/4.00\times10^{-7} = 4.966\times10^{-19}$ J $= 3.100$ eV. Far below 13.6 eV: it cannot ionise ground-state hydrogen. (It could not even excite $1 \to 2$, which needs 10.2 eV.)

**4. Photoelectric effect.** $\text{KE}_{\max} = h\nu - \phi = 3.100 - 2.30 = 0.80$ eV ($1.28\times10^{-19}$ J). Doubling the intensity doubles the photon flux, so twice as many electrons are ejected per second, each still with at most 0.80 eV: the kinetic energy depends on frequency alone. This is the observation that the wave model of light could not explain.

**Alternative valid approaches.** Using $hc = 1240$ eV nm throughout (3.10 eV directly); using the Rydberg form in wavenumbers; quoting 13.6 eV × 96.49 kJ/mol per eV = 1312 kJ/mol. Accept 656 nm, 1312 ± 2 kJ/mol, 0.80 eV. Accept "red" or "visible".

**Common gaps.** Using $1/3^2 - 1/2^2$ and reporting a negative energy without comment (a sign slip, not a conceptual error; ask for the absorbed/emitted distinction); forgetting $N_A$; comparing the 400 nm photon with the work function instead of with $R_H$ in part 3; saying intensity raises the kinetic energy.
