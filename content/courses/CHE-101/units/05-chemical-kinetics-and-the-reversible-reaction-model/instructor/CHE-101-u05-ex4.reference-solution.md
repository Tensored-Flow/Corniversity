## Reference reasoning (instructor only)

Write $A=[\ce{A}]$, $B=[\ce{B}]$, $C=[\ce{C}]$ and $k=k_1+k_2$.

**1. Rate equations and mass balance.** Each step is elementary and first order, so $dA/dt=-k_1A-k_2A=-kA$, $dB/dt=k_1A$ and $dC/dt=k_2A$. Adding the three gives $d(A+B+C)/dt=0$, so $A+B+C=A_0=0.200$ M throughout (constant volume, no other reactions).

**2. Solution.** $A(t)=A_0e^{-kt}$. Then $dB/dt=k_1A_0e^{-kt}$ with $B(0)=0$ integrates to
$$B(t)=\frac{k_1}{k}A_0\big(1-e^{-kt}\big),\qquad C(t)=\frac{k_2}{k}A_0\big(1-e^{-kt}\big).$$
Check: $A+B+C=A_0e^{-kt}+A_0(1-e^{-kt})=A_0$. For every $t>0$, $B/C=k_1/k_2$: both products are made from the same A at rates proportional to it, so they accumulate in a fixed ratio. Each product approaches its final value with the rate constant $k$, the sum, not with its own constant.

**3. Numbers.** Mass balance first: $0.150+0.050=0.200$ M $=A_0$, consistent with no third channel. Had the products fallen short of $A_0$, the two-channel scheme would already be in doubt. The half-life of A is $\ln2/k$, so $k=0.6931/(115.5\ \text{s})=6.00\times10^{-3}$ s⁻¹. The final ratio is $B_\infty/C_\infty=0.150/0.050=3.00=k_1/k_2$. Hence $k_1=\tfrac34k=4.50\times10^{-3}$ s⁻¹ and $k_2=\tfrac14k=1.50\times10^{-3}$ s⁻¹.

**4. What A alone shows.** The slope of $\ln A$ against $t$ is $-(k_1+k_2)$, so the student measured the total rate constant, $6.00\times10^{-3}$ s⁻¹, and not $k_1$. No record of A alone can separate the channels: $A(t)=A_0e^{-(k_1+k_2)t}$ depends on the two constants only through their sum, so every pair with the same sum produces identical A data at all times. A product must be measured: the ratio $B/C$ at any time, or the yield of either product, gives $k_1/k_2$ (or $k_1/k$), and together with the sum that gives each constant.

**5. Temperature.** With $k_i=A_ie^{-E_{a,i}/RT}$ and $A_1/A_2$ independent of $T$,
$$\frac{(k_1/k_2)_{318\,\mathrm{K}}}{(k_1/k_2)_{298\,\mathrm{K}}}=\exp\!\Big[\frac{E_{a,1}-E_{a,2}}{R}\Big(\frac{1}{298}-\frac{1}{318}\Big)\Big]=\exp\!\Big[\frac{10\,000}{8.314}\times2.111\times10^{-4}\Big]=e^{0.254}=1.29 .$$
So $(k_1/k_2)_{318\,\mathrm{K}}=3.00\times1.29=3.87$, and the final concentrations are $B_\infty=0.200\times3.87/4.87=0.159$ M and $C_\infty=0.200/4.87=0.041$ M, which still sum to $0.200$ M. Heating favours $\ce{A -> B}$, the channel with the higher activation energy, because its rate constant is the more sensitive to temperature (in the lesson's Arrhenius model the slope of $\ln k$ against $1/T$ is $-E_a/R$). Both constants rise, so A is also consumed faster.

**Alternative valid approaches.** Solving the linear system by eigenvalues: one eigenvalue $-k$ and a double eigenvalue $0$, matching the two conserved combinations $A+B+C$ and $k_2B-k_1C$. Obtaining $k_1/k_2$ from $B/C$ at a finite time instead of the final values. Working part 5 with the two-temperature form for each rate constant separately, which gives the same ratio.

**Common gaps.** Taking $\ln2/t_{1/2}$ as $k_1$; using the product ratio without first checking the mass balance; writing $B(t)=A_0(1-e^{-k_1t})$, with the wrong rate constant in the exponent and the wrong final value; in part 5 concluding that heating favours the lower-barrier channel; mixing kJ with $R$ in J.
