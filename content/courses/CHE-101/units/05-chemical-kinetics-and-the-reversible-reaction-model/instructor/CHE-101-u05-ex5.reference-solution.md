## Reference reasoning (instructor only)

The table was generated, and rounded to four decimals, from $\ce{A <=>[k_f][k_r] B}$ with $k_f=0.40$ min⁻¹ and $k_r=0.20$ min⁻¹ together with a side step $\ce{B ->[k_s] C}$ with $k_s=0.050$ min⁻¹, starting from $[\ce{A}]_0=1$ M. The learner is not told this; it is recorded here so that estimates can be judged. Write $A$, $B$, $C$ for the concentrations.

**1. Mass balance.** $A+B$ is 1.0000, 0.9919, 0.9731, 0.9489, 0.9225, 0.8951, 0.8676, 0.8406, 0.8141, 0.7884, 0.7633, 0.6492 and 0.5522 M at $t=0,1,\dots,10,15,20$ min. Adding the two rate equations of exercise 3 gives $d(A+B)/dt=0$, so that model requires $A+B=1.0000$ M at every time, whatever $k_f$ and $k_r$ are. The data lose almost half of the material by 20 min, steadily and far beyond rounding. No pair $k_f,k_r$ can fit: the model fails, and the failure is a missing sink, not a wrong pair of constants.

**2. Monotonicity.** In the model, $B=A_0-A$ and $A(t)=A_{eq}+(A_0-A_{eq})e^{-t/\tau}$, with $A_0\ge A_{eq}$ when the run starts from pure A. So A never increases and B never decreases; if $k_f=0$ nothing changes at all. In the data B rises to 0.5502 M at 5 min and then falls (0.5460 M at 6 min, 0.3576 M at 20 min), which the model cannot produce for any $k_f,k_r\ge0$.

**3. Geometric decreases.** With the given form, $A(n)-A(n+1)=(A_0-A_{eq})e^{-n/\tau}\big(1-e^{-1/\tau}\big)$, so consecutive decreases have the constant ratio $e^{-1/\tau}$ (and if $A_0=A_{eq}$ every decrease is zero). The data's decreases from 0 to 10 min are 0.3013, 0.1675, 0.0953, 0.0561, 0.0349, 0.0233, 0.0168, 0.0133, 0.0112 and 0.0100 M, with consecutive ratios 0.556, 0.569, 0.589, 0.622, 0.668, 0.721, 0.792, 0.842 and 0.893. The ratios rise steadily instead of staying constant: the approach is not a single exponential. A fast process dominates early and a slow one late.

**4. The extra step.** For $\ce{A ->[k'] C}$, $d(A+B)/dt=-k'A$; for $\ce{B ->[k_s] C}$, $d(A+B)/dt=-k_sB$. So the loss in each minute, divided by the mean concentration of the feeding species over that minute, should be constant for the right candidate. With trapezoid means:

| Minute | Loss / M | Loss ÷ mean B (min⁻¹) | Loss ÷ mean A (min⁻¹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–1 | 0.0081 | 0.055 | 0.010 |
| 1–2 | 0.0188 | 0.051 | 0.031 |
| 2–3 | 0.0242 | 0.051 | 0.050 |
| 3–4 | 0.0264 | 0.050 | 0.065 |
| 4–5 | 0.0274 | 0.050 | 0.076 |
| 5–6 | 0.0275 | 0.050 | 0.083 |
| 6–7 | 0.0270 | 0.050 | 0.086 |
| 7–8 | 0.0265 | 0.050 | 0.089 |
| 8–9 | 0.0257 | 0.050 | 0.090 |
| 9–10 | 0.0251 | 0.050 | 0.091 |

The quotient by mean A grows nearly tenfold. The quotient by mean B stays at 0.050 min⁻¹, except in the first minute, where B rises steeply and is concave, so the trapezoid mean underestimates its true mean and the quotient comes out too large. The data are consistent with $\ce{B -> C}$, $k_s\approx0.050$ min⁻¹, and not with $\ce{A -> C}$. The corrected equations are
$$\frac{dA}{dt}=-k_fA+k_rB,\qquad\frac{dB}{dt}=k_fA-(k_r+k_s)B,\qquad\frac{dC}{dt}=k_sB,$$
and they conserve $A+B+C=1.0000$ M. The test is to measure C: the diagnosis predicts $C=1-A-B$ at every time (0.4478 M at 20 min) and $dC/dt=k_sB$, so C forms fastest when B is largest, near 5 min. Such a measurement can refute the diagnosis; on its own it cannot exclude every more elaborate scheme.

**5. A steady ratio is not K.** The equation for $dA/dt$ is the same in the corrected model. If $dA/dt<0$ then $k_rB<k_fA$, so $B/A<k_f/k_r$ (for $k_r>0$; if $k_r=0$ the bound says nothing). In the corrected model A is a sum of two decaying exponentials, so $dA/dt$ has at most one zero; it starts negative and A tends to $0^+$, so $dA/dt<0$ at every time and every ratio in the table underestimates $k_f/k_r$. Between 15 and 20 min A falls by the factor $0.1946/0.2288=0.851$ and B by $0.3576/0.4204=0.851$: both are still falling, by the same factor, so their ratio (1.837 at 15 min, 1.838 at 20 min) is steady although neither concentration is. Equilibrium needs constant concentrations, with forward and reverse rates equal; here the net conversion of A to B stays positive and everything drains into C, so there is no equilibrium mixture of A and B whose ratio could be a constant. The reported 1.84 is a lower bound on $k_f/k_r$ (by construction $k_f/k_r=2.00$), not an equilibrium constant. Late in the run only the slower of the system's two exponential modes survives, so A and B decay together as $e^{-\mu t}$ with $\mu\approx0.032$ min⁻¹, and that is why their ratio settles.

**Alternative valid approaches.** Testing for a single exponential with a plot of $\ln(A-A_{last})$, with the caveat that $A_{last}$ is not an equilibrium value. Considering a steady dilution as a third candidate (loss proportional to $A+B$) and rejecting it because the loss divided by the mean of $A+B$ rises from 0.008 to 0.032 min⁻¹ over the first ten minutes; credit this. Fitting the corrected model numerically. Note that the sink cannot be identified from the late minutes alone: there A, B and $A+B$ are proportional, so all the quotients level off and the early minutes carry the evidence.

**Common gaps.** Fitting $k_f$ and $k_r$ without checking the mass balance; reading the maximum in B as noise; identifying the sink from the late minutes, where every quotient is steady; stating that the ratio at 20 min is K because it has stopped changing; claiming that the corrected model reaches an equilibrium with A and B both present.
