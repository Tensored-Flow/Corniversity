## Reference reasoning (instructor only)

Write $A = [\ce{A}]$, $B = [\ce{B}]$.

**1. Conservation and reduction.** Adding the two equations: $d(A + B)/dt = (-k_fA + k_rB) + (k_fA - k_rB) = 0$, so $A + B = A_0 + B_0 = C_0$ for all $t$ (constant volume, no other reactions). Substituting $B = C_0 - A$: $dA/dt = -k_fA + k_r(C_0 - A) = -(k_f + k_r)A + k_rC_0$.

**2. Solution for $k_f+k_r>0$.** Linear, constant coefficients. The fixed point is $A_{eq} = k_rC_0/(k_f + k_r)$; writing $u = A - A_{eq}$, $du/dt = -(k_f + k_r)u$, so $u(t) = u(0)e^{-(k_f + k_r)t}$ and
$$A(t) = A_{eq} + (A_0 - A_{eq})e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{1}{k_f + k_r}.$$
$B_{eq} = C_0 - A_{eq} = k_fC_0/(k_f + k_r)$, and $B_{eq}/A_{eq} = k_f/k_r = K$ when both rates are positive, the equilibrium constant of the stipulated reversible model (lecture 31, page 4: equal forward and reverse rates, $k_fA_{eq} = k_rB_{eq}$). Note that both $k_f$ and $k_r$ appear in $\tau$: the approach is faster than either step alone, because deviation is corrected from both sides.

**3. Numbers.** $k_f + k_r = 0.40$ min$^{-1}$. $A_{eq} = 0.10/0.40 = 0.250$ M; $B_{eq} = 0.750$ M; $K = 3.00$; $\tau = 2.50$ min; half-life of the approach $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/(k_f + k_r) = 1.733$ min (the time for the deviation $A - A_{eq}$ to halve, not for $A$ to halve). At $t = 5.0$ min: $A = 0.250 + 0.750e^{-2.0} = 0.250 + 0.1015 = 0.3515$ M. The simulator integrates the same equations, so it should agree to the displayed precision; a discrepancy indicates a mis-set parameter or a time unit mismatch.

**4. Parameter changes.** $k_f = 0.60$, $k_r = 0.10$: $K = 6$, $A_{eq} = 0.10/0.70 = 0.143$ M, $B_{eq} = 0.857$ M, $\tau = 1/0.70 = 1.43$ min. Both the end point and the speed change. $k_f = 0.60$, $k_r = 0.20$: $K = 3$, $A_{eq} = 0.20/0.80 = 0.250$ M (unchanged), $\tau = 1/0.80 = 1.25$ min. The end point depends on the ratio $k_f/k_r$; the speed depends on the sum $k_f + k_r$. This is the kinetic version of "a catalyst changes the rate, not the equilibrium": in this elementary two-state model a common factor on both constants represents faster equilibration with unchanged K; a general catalytic mechanism need not reduce to that single scaling.

**5. Distinguishing dynamic equilibrium from no reaction.** Any two of: (i) Perturbation: add $\Delta$ of B to the equilibrium mixture. The model predicts relaxation back to $B/A = K$ with time constant $\tau$; a non-reacting mixture stays at the perturbed ratio indefinitely. (ii) Tracer (virtual labelling): tag the A molecules present at one instant. The model predicts the tag appears in B at rate $k_f$ (and returns at $k_r$) while total A and B stay constant; with no reaction the tag never leaves A. (iii) Different starting ratios: start one run at $A_0 = C_0$ and another at $B_0 = C_0$. The model predicts both converge to $B/A = 3$; non-reacting mixtures keep their initial ratios. (iv) Temperature change: if $\Delta H \neq 0$, $K$ changes with $T$ and the composition shifts; a non-reacting mixture does not respond. At equilibrium the net rate is zero while the forward and reverse rates are both $k_fA_{eq} = k_rB_{eq} = 0.075$ M min$^{-1}$.

**6. Assumptions and failures.** Single liquid phase; well mixed (concentrations uniform); constant temperature (so $k_f$, $k_r$ constant); constant volume (so concentrations change only by reaction); dilute, ideal solution (so elementary first-order rate laws in concentration hold); no side reactions or additional species; no transport in or out. Failures: a changing volume adds $-(A/V)\,dV/dt$ to $dA/dt$ and breaks the simple conservation of concentrations (amounts are still conserved); a side reaction $\ce{B -> C}$ adds $-k_cB$ to $dB/dt$, so $A + B$ decays and the system no longer reaches the equilibrium above; poor mixing makes $A$ and $B$ functions of position and requires a diffusion term; a temperature drift makes $k_f$, $k_r$ functions of time (Arrhenius), so $\tau$ and $K$ drift.

**Alternative valid approaches.** Solving by integrating factor; writing the solution in terms of $B(t)$; using the eigenvalues of the $2\times2$ matrix ($0$ and $-(k_f + k_r)$), which is the natural route for this learner and should be welcomed: the zero eigenvalue is the conservation law. Accept any correct pair of distinguishing observations.

**Common gaps.** Claiming the reaction has stopped at equilibrium; giving $\tau = 1/k_f$; forgetting the $k_rC_0$ term after substituting the conservation law; reporting the half-life of $A$ rather than of the deviation; concluding from the doubled-$k_f$ case that "faster means more product" in general.

**Zero-rate boundary cases.** If both rates vanish, every initial composition is stationary; here A(t)=C₀ and B(t)=0, no finite relaxation time or uniquely selected equilibrium ratio follows, and 0/0 is undefined. If kᵣ=0<k_f, A=C₀exp(−k_ft), B=C₀−A, with limiting A=0 and B=C₀; B/A diverges for C₀>0, rather than giving a finite thermodynamic K from a truly reversible model. If k_f=0<kᵣ, the stipulated initially pure A remains A=C₀, B=0; from other starts B would decay into A. Degenerate one-way kinetics should not be confused with finite reversible equilibrium constants. For C₀=0 the empty-system concentration ratios are undefined.

Part 5 is a proposed thought experiment using extensions of the equations, not a claim that the current simulator implements tracers, add-B, arbitrary initial ratios or temperature changes. Only part 3 requires a comparison actually run in the simulator.
