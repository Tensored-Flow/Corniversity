## Reference reasoning (instructor only)

**Part A.**

**1. Orders.** Runs 1 → 2: $[\ce{NO}]$ doubles at fixed $[\ce{Cl2}]$; rate goes $1.8\times10^{-4} \to 7.2\times10^{-4}$, a factor of $4 = 2^2$, so $m = 2$. Runs 1 → 3: $[\ce{Cl2}]$ doubles at fixed $[\ce{NO}]$; rate doubles, so $n = 1$.

**2. Rate law and $k$.** $\text{rate} = k[\ce{NO}]^2[\ce{Cl2}]$. From run 1: $k = 1.8\times10^{-4}/(0.10^2\times0.10) = 1.8\times10^{-4}/1.0\times10^{-3} = 0.18$ M$^{-2}$ s$^{-1}$. (Run 2: $7.2\times10^{-4}/(0.04\times0.10) = 0.18$; run 3: $3.6\times10^{-4}/(0.01\times0.20) = 0.18$. Consistent.) Units: M s$^{-1}$ divided by M$^3$.

**3. Why experiment was needed.** Orders are empirical; they coincide with stoichiometric coefficients only for a single elementary step. The data happen to match the coefficients here, but that neither proves a termolecular collision nor could have been assumed; exercise 2 shows a two-step mechanism that gives the same law. (The data are constructed for the exercise and are not measurements.)

**Part B.**

**4. First-order times.** $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/k = 0.6931/2.50\times10^{-3} = 277$ s. Time to 10%: $\ln(0.10) = -kt \Rightarrow t = \ln 10/k = 2.3026/2.50\times10^{-3} = 921$ s. Both depend only on $k$ (hence on temperature), not on the initial concentration; that independence is the signature of first order. (For a second-order process both would depend on $[\ce{A}]_0$.)

**Alternative valid approaches.** Taking logarithms of rate ratios ($m = \ln 4/\ln 2$); computing $k$ from any run. Accept $k = 0.18$ with units written as $\text{L}^2\,\text{mol}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}$. Accept 277–278 s and 921 s.

**Common gaps.** Reading orders from coefficients and stopping there; omitting units of $k$ or giving s$^{-1}$; using the second-order half-life formula; claiming the time to 10% is "three half-lives" ($3 \times 277 = 832$ s is the time to 12.5%, not 10%).
