# Synthesis: one equilibrium, several routes to understanding

The final unit asks you to build and audit a chemical argument from the beginning. Rather than recall a separate equation for each chapter, decide which conserved quantities, state functions and kinetic models describe the question. MIT lecture 35 is a source example of connecting bonding, thermodynamics, acid–base chemistry and transition metals in one chemical story. Our assessment uses its own simplified model systems and explicitly supplied numerical data.

## 1. State the system before selecting an equation

A defensible report begins with a reaction equation and a boundary: which matter can enter or leave, which reservoir fixes temperature or pH, and whether the experiment is closed, flowing or electrically driven. Closed equilibrium is a different problem from a buffered solution maintained by an external reservoir, or a cell with imposed voltage. A catalyst, a buffer and an electrical supply change different parts of the model.

Use a species table containing amount, concentration or activity, phase, charge and any conserved element. Conservation constrains every later calculation. For a closed reaction, write an extent ξ: n_i=n_i,0+ν_iξ. The allowed interval of ξ keeps all amounts nonnegative. With more than one reaction, use one extent per independent reaction and preserve the same elemental balances.

For a simple ideal A ⇌ B at constant volume, total C=A+B is conserved. If K=4, the equilibrium concentrations satisfy B/A=4, hence A=C/5 and B=4C/5. These fractions do not depend on the speed of interconversion. They do depend on the selected reaction, ideality and temperature. If B is removed continuously, the closed-system calculation no longer describes the apparatus.

## 2. Reconcile thermodynamics and kinetics without overextending either

Thermodynamics gives ΔrG=RT ln(Q/K). It predicts the direction of spontaneous change of the selected reaction at the stated composition. At equilibrium Q=K and net affinity vanishes. This is a statement about reaction free energy, not that the total Gibbs energy of the system is zero.

For an elementary first-order A ⇌ B model, the same equilibrium follows from k_f A=k_r B, giving K=k_f/k_r. The relaxation rate is k_f+k_r. Thus endpoint data constrain a ratio and time-course data constrain a sum. Combining the two can identify both rates within this particular model. An observed overall reaction need not have mass-action exponents equal to its displayed stoichiometric coefficients: a multi-step mechanism must be justified separately.

Standard-state factors matter when the stoichiometries differ. For an ideal elementary A ⇌ 2B, k_f[A]=k_r[B]² yields [B]²/[A]=k_f/k_r, whose units are concentration. The dimensionless thermodynamic constant is K=([B]/c°)²/([A]/c°)=(k_f/k_r)/c°. This is why “K is always the ratio of rate constants” without units or a rate-law convention is incomplete.

## 3. Couple equilibria by adding reactions

When reactions add, their standard free energies add and their equilibrium constants multiply. If one reaction is reversed, its K is inverted; if all coefficients are multiplied by m, K is raised to m. This rule connects acid–base, solubility, complexation and redox equilibria without inventing a different thermodynamics for each topic.

Consider a hypothetical sparingly soluble MA(s) ⇌ M⁺+A⁻ with Ksp, and acid dissociation HA ⇌ H⁺+A⁻ with Ka. Adding dissolution to the reverse acid reaction gives MA(s)+H⁺ ⇌ M⁺+HA, whose K is Ksp/Ka. Acid can increase total dissolution by binding A⁻ as HA. It does not change the original Ksp at fixed temperature. If an external buffer fixes h=[H⁺], the dissolved fraction in A⁻ is α=Ka/(Ka+h/c°). Here Ka is dimensionless and h/c° is the proton activity in the ideal concentration approximation. With no other dissolved ions or complexes from this salt, [M⁺]=s and [A⁻]=αs, so in a concentration approximation s≈√(Ksp(c°)²/α). This equation needs a remaining solid phase and a genuinely maintained pH. In an unbuffered system, solve the proton and charge balances too.

This example captures a recurring pattern: a measured total concentration can change because speciation changes while the same fundamental equilibrium constants remain valid. Do not insert a total analytical concentration where the quotient requires free-species activity.

## 4. Energy input and catalytic assistance are different interventions

A reduction half reaction involving two protons and two electrons has a pH-dependent equilibrium potential. For CO₂+2H⁺+2e⁻→HCOOH, Q=a(HCOOH)/(a(CO₂)a(H⁺)²). At 298.15 K, increasing pH by one at fixed other activities lowers its reduction potential by about 0.05916 V. If product acid dissociates, its neutral activity also changes; the stipulation “fixed other activities” must be honoured when interpreting that slope.

A catalyst can help a finite current occur with smaller kinetic losses. It does not remove the free-energy requirement for an uphill overall reaction. An electrical supply can provide that energy. An enzyme coupled to another favourable reaction can also drive an otherwise unfavourable transformation, but then the overall reaction includes the coupled chemistry and its equilibrium constant is different from the uncoupled reaction's.

The atomic and bonding ideas from early units remain relevant. Carbon in CO₂ is formally +4; in formic acid it is +2. Two electrons are needed per carbon converted. CO₂ is linear in a basic Lewis/VSEPR model and nonpolar despite polar bonds. Formic acid contains distinct bonding and lone-pair environments; geometry and acid speciation help determine which molecular state belongs in a reaction quotient. A formal electron count is useful without asserting that all bonds are purely ionic.

## 5. Design evidence at the scale of the claim

A conversion claim needs an amount of identified product, not just current. A catalytic claim needs a baseline and evidence for turnover or regeneration. An equilibrium claim needs reversibility, composition measurements and sufficient time, not a single flat trace. A structural claim needs evidence capable of distinguishing candidate structures. Measurements should include blanks, calibration, uncertainty and a stated observation window.

A good report can conclude that the model is inadequate. If mass is missing, locate the unmeasured species or boundary flow before announcing a violation of conservation. If a rate curve changes with time, distinguish depletion, temperature drift and catalyst deactivation. If a fitted parameter changes across conditions, check whether the same species and reaction mechanism still apply.

## 6. Assessment blueprint and study method

The sheet first tests thermodynamic/kinetic consistency, then coupled speciation, then quantitative experimental design. Its final capstone covers the course chain: electron and atom bookkeeping; bonding/geometry; thermodynamic direction and pH dependence; Faraday stoichiometry; coordination models; kinetic versus equilibrium claims; and a falsifiable measurement plan. Use notes and source pages as references, but write the argument yourself and record assistance.

For the final report allocate roughly 20% of effort to the species/reaction model, 35% to calculations with units and independent checks, 25% to interpreting evidence and competing explanations, and 20% to a coherent experimental argument. The exercise rubric assigns equal weights to its six stated criteria; these effort suggestions are not a different grading formula. Revisit one calculation without notes on another day and explain an alternative limiting case aloud. The recorded final exercise is broad evidence for integration; it does not erase earlier gaps or certify mastery of every detail in the course.
