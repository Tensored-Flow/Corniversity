# Equal pH does not mean equal buffer capacity — reference reasoning

Both initial ratios are $2/3$, giving pH $4.70+\log_{10}(2/3)=4.5239$ under the buffer approximation. In P the acid leaves $n_{A^-}=0.0150$ mol and $n_{HA}=0.0350$ mol, so pH $=4.70+\log_{10}(3/7)=4.3320$. In Q only $0.00200$ mol A$^-$ was available, so it is exhausted and $0.00300$ mol strong acid remains: its concentration is $0.00600$ M. The weak acid total is $0.0100$ M. Leading pH is $-\log_{10}0.00600=2.2218$. More accurately let its extra dissociation be $x$: $K_a=(0.00600+x)x/(0.0100-x)$, with $K_a=10^{-4.70}$. The root is about $3.30\times10^{-5}$ M, giving pH about $2.2195$. Both computations must keep the spectator counterions in the underlying charge balance. Actual activity coefficients and final volume, temperature and analytical composition would be required for nonideal accuracy. Ratio controls approximate pH; absolute inventory controls resistance to additions.
